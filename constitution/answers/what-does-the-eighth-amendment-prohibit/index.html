What does the Eighth Amendment prohibit?

The Eighth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution reads, in full: "Excessive bail shall not be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel and unusual punishments inflicted." Its three clauses limit government power at three different stages — setting bail before trial, imposing fines as punishment, and sentencing after conviction — but the "cruel and unusual punishments" clause has generated by far the most litigation. Unlike many constitutional provisions, the Supreme Court has explicitly said this clause is not fixed to founding-era understanding. In Trop v. Dulles (1958), Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote that the Eighth Amendment "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society" — treating cruelty as a judgment that can shift as social consensus shifts, rather than a fixed 1791 checklist. That evolving-standards approach shaped the Court's most consequential Eighth Amendment ruling, Furman v. Georgia (1972), which struck down every existing state death penalty statute — not by declaring capital punishment inherently cruel, but by finding that the way it was actually being imposed was arbitrary, capricious, and fell disproportionately on Black defendants and the poor, making it functionally random rather than a reasoned application of law. States responded by rewriting their statutes with narrower sentencing guidelines, and the Court allowed capital punishment to resume in Gregg v. Georgia (1976) once those safeguards were in place. The clause has since been extended well beyond the death penalty, applying to prison conditions, sentence length relative to the crime, and punishments imposed on juveniles.
Source: U.S. Const. amend. VIII; Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86 (1958); Furman v. Georgia, 408 U.S. 238 (1972)
