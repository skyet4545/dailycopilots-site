What is the Origination Clause?

The Origination Clause is Article I, Section 7, Clause 1 of the U.S. Constitution. It reads: "All Bills for raising Revenue shall originate in the House of Representatives; but the Senate may propose or concur with Amendments as on other Bills." The rule requires that any bill whose primary purpose is to raise tax revenue must start in the House rather than the Senate, though the Senate retains full power to amend such a bill once it arrives, including rewriting it substantially. The clause traces back to English parliamentary practice, where the House of Commons, the chamber elected by and answerable to ordinary subjects, held the exclusive right to originate money bills, while the unelected House of Lords could only accept or reject them. At the Constitutional Convention, the same logic applied to the new House of Representatives, the chamber apportioned by population and elected directly by voters every two years, in contrast to the Senate, whose members were originally chosen by state legislatures rather than by popular vote. Putting the power to originate tax legislation in the House was meant to tie the "power of the purse" to the body structurally closest to the people who would pay the taxes. In practice, the clause has generated less litigation than its symbolic weight might suggest, mainly because the Senate's amendment power is broad enough that it can functionally rewrite a House revenue bill almost entirely and still satisfy the origination requirement, so long as the bill nominally started in the House. Courts, including the Supreme Court in Flint v. Stone Tracy Co. (1911) and United States v. Munoz-Flores (1990), have read the clause narrowly, applying it only to bills whose central purpose is raising revenue for general government funding, not to bills that merely happen to include a fee, penalty, or incidental revenue provision as part of a broader regulatory scheme. The Senate has also developed a practice of taking an unrelated House-passed bill, replacing its entire text with a revenue measure, and passing it back, a technique sometimes called a "shell bill," which has been consistently upheld as satisfying the clause's technical origination requirement.
Source: U.S. Constitution, Art. I, Sec. 7, Cl. 1; Flint v. Stone Tracy Co. (1911); United States v. Munoz-Flores (1990)
