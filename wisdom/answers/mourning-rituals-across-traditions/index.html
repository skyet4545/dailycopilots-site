How do different traditions structure mourning after a death?

Traditions differ sharply not on whether grief is real, but on how it should be structured. Jewish practice builds mourning into defined stages: shiva, the intense seven-day period immediately following burial, during which mourners remain at home, are prohibited from work, grooming, and ordinary activity, and are visited by community members who fulfill the specific mitzvah of comforting the bereaved; this is followed by the thirty-day shloshim and, for a parent, a fuller year of mourning — a staged de-escalation rather than an open-ended process. The emphasis throughout is communal: mourners are not expected to manage grief alone, and the community's obligation to show up is treated as seriously as the mourner's obligation to observe the restrictions. Buddhist funeral practice, particularly in traditions observing a 49-day cycle, is oriented differently — toward the deceased's transition rather than primarily the survivors' processing. Monks lead chanting and rituals intended to generate merit that supports a favorable rebirth, framed within the broader Buddhist teaching on impermanence (anicca): death is treated as a natural transition within an ongoing cycle rather than a final, singular loss, which shapes the ritual's tone. Stoic consolation took a third, non-ritual form entirely: Seneca wrote extended personal letters of consolation — to his own mother Helvia, to a woman named Marcia, and to Polybius — that engaged grief through reasoned argument rather than prescribed practice, acknowledging grief as natural for a period while urging the reader toward a rational reframing of loss as part of nature's larger, impersonal order. Where Judaism structures grief through community and ritual stages, and Buddhism orients it toward the deceased's ongoing journey, Stoicism treats it as primarily a problem for the individual mind to work through by argument.
Source: Chabad.org, "Shiva and Other Mourning Observances"; Seneca, Consolation to Marcia; Consolation to Helvia
