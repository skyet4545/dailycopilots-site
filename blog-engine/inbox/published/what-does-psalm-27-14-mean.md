**Short answer:** Psalm 27:14 calls the believer to actively trust God through a delay: "Wait on the LORD: be of good courage, and he shall strengthen thine heart: wait, I say, on the LORD" (KJV). Waiting here is not passive killing of time — it is confident expectation of God's action, and the promise attached is inner strength while you wait, not necessarily a quick answer.

## The context: David under pressure

Psalm 27 is attributed to David, and it moves in two distinct halves. Verses 1–6 are soaring confidence: "The LORD is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear?" (v. 1, KJV). David pictures enemies stumbling, his head lifted above them, and himself dwelling in God's house.

Then the tone shifts. Verses 7–12 are urgent petition — hide not thy face, leave me not, deliver me not to the will of my adversaries. He mentions false witnesses rising up against him (v. 12) and even the possibility of being forsaken by father and mother (v. 10).

Verse 14 is the closing word after that plea. It is what David says to himself — and to us — when the confidence of verse 1 has not yet been visibly vindicated. That placement matters: this is not advice from someone whose problem is already solved.

## What it means, phrase by phrase

**"Wait on the LORD."** The Hebrew idea behind waiting in this psalm carries the sense of hopeful, tensed expectation — like a rope drawn taut. It is directed at a Person, not an outcome. You wait *on the LORD*, not merely for relief.

**"Be of good courage."** Waiting is treated as something that requires bravery. That is honest. Delay tempts us toward panic, toward manufacturing our own rescue, or toward quiet despair. Scripture treats holding steady as an act of courage.

**"And he shall strengthen thine heart."** This is the promise — and notice carefully what it promises. Not that the enemies vanish tomorrow. Not that the answer arrives on your timeline. It promises that God will make your inner life sturdy in the meantime. The heart in Hebrew thought is the seat of will, thought, and resolve; God strengthens the very faculty that waiting exhausts.

**"Wait, I say, on the LORD."** The repetition is deliberate. David says it twice because once is not enough — for him or for the reader. It reads like a man steadying himself out loud.

## Cross-references

- **Isaiah 40:31** — they that wait upon the LORD renew their strength, mounting up with wings as eagles.
- **Psalm 31:24** — nearly identical wording: "Be of good courage, and he shall strengthen your heart, all ye that hope in the LORD" (KJV).
- **Psalm 130:5–6** — waiting for the Lord more than watchmen wait for the morning.
- **Lamentations 3:25–26** — the LORD is good to those who wait for Him.
- **Galatians 6:9** — in due season we shall reap, if we faint not.
- **Romans 8:25** — if we hope for what we don't see, we wait for it with patience.

## How to apply it today

Waiting is the ordinary Christian condition — for a diagnosis, a job, a reconciliation, a prodigal, an unanswered prayer that is years old. Psalm 27:14 gives three practical handles.

First, aim your waiting at God, not at the outcome. The difference is whether your peace collapses when the timeline moves.

Second, ask for the promise the verse actually makes. Pray for a strengthened heart *inside* the delay rather than only for the delay to end. That is a prayer this verse gives you warrant to expect God to answer.

Third, preach to yourself like David does. He repeats the command because feelings do not naturally hold the line. Saying the verse out loud, writing it where you'll see it, or returning to it daily is not sentimentality — it's the pattern the psalm itself models.

Faithful Christians will note that Scripture also commends decisive action (Nehemiah prayed *and* set a guard). Waiting on God is not a blanket excuse for inaction; it is refusing to move ahead of God in anxiety while remaining willing to move when He makes the way clear. Where wisdom is unclear, James 1:5 invites you to ask for it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does "wait on the LORD" mean do nothing?**
No. Biblical waiting is active — it involves prayer, obedience in the meantime, and expectation. The word pictures leaning forward in hope, not sitting idle. David wrote this psalm while still pleading with God and still surrounded by adversaries, not while passively withdrawn from his situation.

**How long does God expect us to wait?**
Scripture doesn't give a timeline, and it repeatedly features long waits — Abraham for a son, Israel in exile, the psalmists crying "how long?" (Psalm 13:1). The promise in Psalm 27:14 is strength during the wait rather than a guaranteed duration.

**What does "he shall strengthen thine heart" mean?**
In Hebrew usage the heart is the center of will, thought, and courage. The promise is that God fortifies your resolve and inner steadiness — the exact thing that erodes under prolonged uncertainty — rather than promising to remove the pressure.

**Is Psalm 27:14 a promise that God will give me what I asked for?**
It isn't stated that way. The verse promises God's strengthening presence to those who wait on Him. Elsewhere Scripture affirms God answers prayer (1 John 5:14) while also making clear that His timing and His answers may differ from ours (Isaiah 55:8–9).
