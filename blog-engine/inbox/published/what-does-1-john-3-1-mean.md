**Short answer:** 1 John 3:1 calls readers to stop and stare at something astonishing — that God's love has gone so far as to *name* believers his own children — and then explains a side effect: because the world never recognized Jesus, it does not recognize those who belong to him either.

The verse reads, "Behold, what manner of love the Father hath bestowed upon us, that we should be called the sons of God: therefore the world knoweth us not, because it knew him not" (1 John 3:1, KJV).

## The context

John writes to believers unsettled by people who had left their fellowship (1 John 2:19) and by teaching that denied essential truths about Jesus (2:22–23; 4:2–3). Much of the letter gives readers grounds for assurance — tests of belief, love, and obedience by which they can know where they stand (5:13).

Chapter 2 ends with a statement about everyone who does righteousness being born of God (2:29). Chapter 3 opens by drawing out what that new birth means, and John cannot get to the doctrine without first pausing over the wonder of it. Verse 2 continues immediately: "now are we the sons of God, and it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is" (KJV).

## What it means, phrase by phrase

**"Behold."** An imperative to look. John is not making an argument here so much as directing attention. The structure of the sentence assumes the reader has become too used to this.

**"What manner of love."** The Greek term behind this phrase carries a sense of *what sort of* — something out of the ordinary, even foreign. It is the language of surprise. John is not measuring the love's size but marveling at its kind.

**"The Father hath bestowed upon us."** The verb indicates a completed gift. This is not love the readers are working toward but love already given.

**"That we should be called the sons of God."** The word "called" is important. Believers are not merely *treated as if* they were children; God names them so, and in Scripture God's naming establishes reality. Many manuscripts add a short phrase here — "and such we are" — which several modern translations include; the KJV follows a manuscript tradition without it. Either way, verse 2 states it outright: "now are we the sons of God."

**"Therefore the world knoweth us not, because it knew him not."** John connects two failures of recognition. The world did not recognize Jesus (John 1:10–11); it will not recognize those identified with him. This reframes feeling out of place — not as evidence something has gone wrong, but as a consequence of family resemblance.

## Cross-references

- 1 John 3:2 — the future side: "we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is."
- John 1:10–12 — the world did not know him, but to those who received him he gave the right to become children of God.
- Romans 8:14–17 — the Spirit of adoption, crying "Abba, Father," and heirs with Christ.
- Galatians 4:4–7 — no longer a servant but a son, and an heir through God.
- John 15:18–19 — "If the world hate you, ye know that it hated me before it hated you" (KJV).
- Ephesians 1:4–5 — predestined "unto the adoption of children."

Christians hold different views on how sonship comes about — some emphasizing adoption as a legal act, some emphasizing the new birth as a change of nature, most affirming both — and they differ over the relationship between God's choosing and human response. Those debates draw on passages like Ephesians 1:4–5 and John 1:12. What 1 John 3:1 asserts is not contested among them: the status is a gift of the Father's love, not an achievement.

## How to apply it today

The verse's first instruction is not to do anything — it is to look. That has practical force for anyone whose relationship with God has become mostly a set of obligations. Spend a moment on the title itself: *child of God*. John clearly expected it to be startling.

Second, use it where identity is under pressure. Much of modern anxiety runs on performance and standing. This verse locates standing in a name the Father gave, described in verse 1 as already bestowed.

Third, reinterpret the sense of not fitting in. John says plainly that being unrecognized by the world follows from Christ being unrecognized. That will not make the experience pleasant, but it does change what it means. And verse 2 adds the horizon: what you are now is real, and what you will be is not yet visible.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What does "what manner of love" mean?**
The Greek phrase carries a sense of astonishment at the *kind* of love, not just its amount — something unfamiliar or from another country. John is inviting readers to find it strange rather than routine. That is why the verse opens with "Behold."

**Are all people children of God according to this verse?**
1 John uses the term for those born of God through faith in Christ, and the letter draws a clear line between God's children and the world (1 John 3:10; 5:1). Scripture elsewhere affirms that all people are God's creation and bear his image (Genesis 1:27; Acts 17:28). Christian traditions weight these truths differently, but 1 John 3:1 is specifically about the relationship given to believers.

**Why does the world not know us?**
John gives the reason in the verse itself: it did not know Christ. The lack of recognition is inherited from him, echoing John 1:10 and Jesus's warning in John 15:18–19. Feeling out of step with the surrounding culture is presented as expected, not as a malfunction.

**How does 1 John 3:1 connect to verse 2?**
Verse 1 states the present reality; verse 2 adds the unfinished part. Believers are children of God *now*, but "it doth not yet appear what we shall be" (KJV) — with the promise that when Christ appears, we will be like him. Together they hold present identity and future transformation in the same breath.
