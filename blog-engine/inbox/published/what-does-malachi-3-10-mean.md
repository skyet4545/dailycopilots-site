**Short answer:** Malachi 3:10 calls a nation that had been withholding its tithes to bring them fully into the temple storehouse, and invites them to test whether God will not respond with overwhelming provision. It is a covenant challenge to Israel about restored faithfulness in giving — one Christians apply in differing ways.

## The context: an accusation of robbery

Malachi is structured as a dispute. God makes a charge, the people object, and God answers. In chapter 3 the charge is blunt: "Will a man rob God? Yet ye have robbed me. But ye say, Wherein have we robbed thee? In tithes and offerings" (Malachi 3:8, KJV).

The setting is post-exilic Judah with a functioning but neglected temple. Earlier chapters describe priests offering blemished animals (1:6–8) and treating the altar's service as a burden (1:13). Nehemiah 13:10–12 records a parallel situation: the Levites' portions had not been supplied, so the temple staff abandoned their posts and went back to their fields. When the tithe stopped, the worship system stopped.

Verse 9 says the nation is under a curse. Verse 10 is the way back.

## What the verse means, phrase by phrase

"Bring ye all the tithes into the storehouse, that there may be meat in mine house, and prove me now herewith, saith the LORD of hosts, if I will not open you the windows of heaven, and pour you out a blessing, that there shall not be room enough to receive it" (Malachi 3:10, KJV).

**"all the tithes"** — The emphasis is on *all*. Partial compliance was the problem. In the Mosaic law the tithe supported the Levites, the festivals, and the poor (Numbers 18:21–24, Deuteronomy 14:22–29), so shortfalls had immediate human consequences.

**"into the storehouse, that there may be meat in mine house"** — "Meat" in older English simply means food. The storehouse was the temple's supply room. The purpose named here is functional: so that there is provision in God's house. Giving is tied to a working institution and the people it feeds.

**"prove me now herewith"** — "Prove" means *test*. This is striking, because Deuteronomy 6:16 forbids testing God. Here God invites it — an offer extended to a specific people in a specific covenant relationship.

**"the windows of heaven"** — The same phrase appears in the flood narrative (Genesis 7:11) and in 2 Kings 7:2, where a skeptical officer doubts God could supply food even if He made windows in heaven. For an agrarian society, windows of heaven meant rain, and rain meant harvest. The immediate promise in verses 11–12 is agricultural: the devourer rebuked, the vine not casting its fruit.

## Cross-references

- **Malachi 3:8–9, 11–12** — the charge and the full promise, including the rebuke of the devourer.
- **Numbers 18:21–24; Deuteronomy 14:22–29** — how the tithe functioned in Israel.
- **Nehemiah 13:10–12** — the same crisis of unsupplied Levites.
- **2 Corinthians 9:6–8** — "God loveth a cheerful giver" (KJV); giving as purposed in the heart, not by quota.
- **2 Corinthians 8:12–14** — giving according to ability, aiming at equality.
- **Luke 6:38** — good measure, pressed down, running over.
- **1 Timothy 6:17–19** — instruction to the rich, with no tithe percentage named.

## How to apply it today

This is one of the passages where honest Christians land in different places, and it is worth stating the range plainly rather than picking a winner.

Many churches teach the tithe as an ongoing standard, reading Malachi 3:10 as a durable principle: give the full tenth first, and trust God's provision. They point to the tithe's presence before the law (Genesis 14:20, Genesis 28:22) and to Jesus's remark in Matthew 23:23 that the Pharisees should have practiced justice and mercy *without* neglecting the tithe.

Others hold that the New Testament deliberately drops the percentage. Paul's fundraising chapters, 2 Corinthians 8–9, never mention a tenth; they call for proportional, voluntary, cheerful giving, and warn against giving "grudgingly, or of necessity" (9:7). On this reading, Malachi's promise belongs to Israel's covenant with its temple economy, and the enduring principle is generosity rather than a fixed rate.

Two things both sides generally agree on. First, the verse is not a formula for personal wealth. The blessing promised in verses 11–12 is crops and national reputation, and using it as an investment pitch misrepresents it. Second, whatever the percentage, withholding from God's work while claiming to worship Him is what Malachi calls robbery — and that indictment translates directly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does Malachi 3:10 promise financial prosperity if I tithe?**
The promise in its context is agricultural and national — rain, protected crops, and a good name among the nations (vv. 11–12). Treating it as a guarantee of personal financial gain reads more into the text than it says, and Scripture elsewhere warns against making godliness a means of profit (1 Timothy 6:5–10).

**Are Christians required to tithe ten percent?**
Christians disagree. Some hold the tithe as a continuing standard, citing its pre-law appearances and Matthew 23:23; others hold that the New Testament replaces a fixed percentage with proportional, cheerful giving, citing 2 Corinthians 8–9, which never names a tenth. Both positions are held by serious believers.

**Why does God invite Israel to test Him here when Deuteronomy 6:16 forbids it?**
Because this is God's own invitation rather than human presumption. The prohibition in Deuteronomy addresses people demanding proof on their own terms; Malachi 3:10 records God offering a specific test to a specific covenant people about a specific act of obedience.

**What was the "storehouse"?**
It was the temple's supply room, where tithed grain, oil, and wine were kept to support the Levites and the temple's work. Nehemiah 13:10–12 shows what happened when it ran empty: the Levites left their service to farm.
