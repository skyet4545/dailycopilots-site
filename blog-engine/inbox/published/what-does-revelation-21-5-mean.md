**Short answer:** Revelation 21:5 says, "And he that sat upon the throne said, Behold, I make all things new. And he said unto me, Write: for these words are true and faithful" (KJV). It is one of the few places in Revelation where God speaks directly from the throne. The promise is not that God makes *new things* — it is that he makes *all things new*: the existing creation restored rather than discarded.

## The context: after the tears are wiped away

Revelation 21 opens the final vision of the book. John sees "a new heaven and a new earth" (21:1), and the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down from God (21:2). A great voice announces that God will dwell with humanity — "he will dwell with them, and they shall be his people" (21:3, KJV).

Verse 4 is the promise most readers know: God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes, and "there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away" (KJV).

Verse 5 is God's own confirmation of all of it. Throughout Revelation, the voice from the throne is rare; most of the book is mediated through angels, elders, and living creatures. Here the one seated on the throne speaks in the first person.

The setting matters for how the book was first received. Revelation was written to churches under pressure — facing hostility, economic exclusion, and in some cases death. Chapter 21 is not a decorative epilogue. It is the answer to everything the churches were enduring.

## What it means, phrase by phrase

**"He that sat upon the throne."** The throne has been the fixed center of the book since chapter 4. The one who speaks is the sovereign over everything the previous chapters described.

**"Behold."** A summons to look. In a book saturated with terrifying imagery, this is where attention is demanded for something else.

**"I make all things new."** The word order is the interpretive key. Not "I make all new things" but "I make all things new." The Greek word for "new" here (*kainos*) tends to carry the sense of new in quality or character rather than merely recent in time. The object is *all things* — the same creation, renewed.

The verb is present tense — "I am making" — which some read as emphasizing certainty, and others as pointing to a renewal already underway in Christ, consummated at the end. Both readings appear in the history of interpretation.

**"Write: for these words are true and faithful."** John is commanded to record it, and the reason given is the reliability of the words. The same pair of terms describes Christ himself in Revelation 19:11 as "Faithful and True" (KJV). The guarantee of the promise is the character of the one making it.

**Where faithful Christians differ:** whether the new heaven and new earth means the present creation is annihilated and replaced, or purified and renewed. Those emphasizing replacement point to 2 Peter 3:10–12 and the dissolving of the elements. Those emphasizing renewal point to Romans 8:19–22, where creation itself is delivered from bondage, and to the wording of this very verse. Christians also differ over how literally to read Revelation's imagery — futurist, historicist, preterist, and idealist approaches all exist within orthodox Christianity — while agreeing on the substance of the hope.

## How to apply it today

Let the verb be transitive. The promise is not that you will get a different life somewhere else, but that God restores what is broken. That reframes grief. The losses are not written off; they are named as things God intends to undo.

Second, notice that verse 5 follows verse 4 rather than preceding it. God addresses the tears first. Whatever this hope is, it is not a demand that suffering be minimized. Revelation is unflinching about how bad things get.

Third, take the closing clause personally. "These words are true and faithful" was written for people who had every visible reason to doubt it. When circumstances argue otherwise, the ground of confidence stays the same as it was for them — the character of the speaker, not the state of the evidence.

Finally, if renewal is God's declared direction for creation, it is worth asking whether your work, relationships, and care for the world run with that grain or against it. 2 Corinthians 5:17 says the new creation has already begun in those who are in Christ.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does Revelation 21:5 mean God destroys the world and makes a different one?**
Christians read this differently. The wording — "all things new" rather than "all new things" — leads many to understand it as the renewal of the existing creation, supported by Romans 8:19–22. Others emphasize passages like 2 Peter 3:10–12 that describe the elements dissolving, and read it as replacement. Both views affirm that the outcome is a creation free from death and corruption.

**Who is speaking in Revelation 21:5?**
The one seated on the throne — God himself. This is notable because most of Revelation is communicated through angels, elders, or the living creatures. Direct first-person speech from the throne appears only a few times in the book, which gives this declaration particular weight.

**Why does the verse say "these words are true and faithful"?**
Revelation was written to churches facing persecution, where the promise of restored creation would have seemed contradicted by daily experience. The assurance grounds the promise in the character of the speaker — the same two words describe Christ in Revelation 19:11 — rather than in what the readers could see.

**Is the "newness" here about time or about quality?**
The Greek word *kainos* generally emphasizes newness of kind or character rather than simply recent origin. The point is not that these things did not exist before, but that they are made fresh, restored, and freed from decay. That fits the surrounding promise that death, sorrow, and pain have passed away.
