**Short answer:** Proverbs 11:25 observes that generous people tend to flourish, using the image of a farmer who irrigates other fields and finds his own watered too. It is a wisdom observation about how generosity works over a lifetime, not a transaction that guarantees a payout.

## The context: a cluster on giving and hoarding

Proverbs 11:25 belongs to the long collection of two-line sayings that runs from chapter 10 through chapter 22, traditionally attributed to Solomon. Most of these couplets set two ways of living side by side.

Verse 25 does not stand alone — it sits in a deliberate cluster. Verse 24 says there is one who scatters and yet increases, and one who withholds more than is right and ends in poverty. Verse 26 warns about the grain dealer who hoards while people go hungry. So the immediate subject is real economic behavior in an agrarian village, where withholding food during scarcity was a live temptation and generosity had visible consequences.

## What it means, phrase by phrase

The King James Version reads: **"The liberal soul shall be made fat: and he that watereth shall be watered also himself"** (Proverbs 11:25).

- **"The liberal soul"** — Older English for the *generous* person. The Hebrew is literally something like "a soul of blessing" — a person who is a source of blessing to others, not merely someone who occasionally gives.
- **"shall be made fat"** — Jarring to modern ears, but in a subsistence economy fatness meant health, abundance, and having margin. The modern equivalent is *will flourish* or *will thrive*.
- **"he that watereth shall be watered also himself"** — The second line switches to irrigation. A farmer who opens his channels to neighboring plots finds water coming back to his own. This is the mechanism the proverb is describing: generosity circulates.

Notice what the verse does *not* say. There is no stated timeline, no promised amount, and no mention of praying for a return. Proverbs deals in general patterns of how the world usually goes, which is why the same book can also say the righteous sometimes suffer and the wicked sometimes prosper.

## How to apply Proverbs 11:25 today

The honest application is habitual rather than transactional. People who build generosity into their lives — money, time, attention, credit for good work — usually find themselves surrounded by people who do the same for them. That is the pattern the proverb names.

It is worth being candid about the misuse. This verse is sometimes quoted to imply that giving is an investment strategy with a guaranteed return, especially a financial one. Faithful Christians disagree about how much material blessing to expect: some traditions read verses like this and 2 Corinthians 9:8 as promising real provision, while others read the return primarily as spiritual and relational, noting that Paul himself knew hunger and want (Philippians 4:12). Proverbs' own genre argues for humility here — it describes the grain of the universe, not a lever you pull.

A safer way to hold it: give because generosity is good and God is generous, and let the flourishing be a byproduct rather than the motive. The moment the watering becomes a strategy for getting watered, the proverb has been turned inside out.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Proverbs 11:25 a promise of financial return?** It is a wisdom observation about a general pattern, not a contractual guarantee. Proverbs frequently states what usually happens rather than what always happens. Christians differ on how materially to read the flourishing, and the verse itself does not specify.

**What does "made fat" mean?** In the ancient agrarian world, fatness signified health, abundance, and having enough to spare. Modern translations usually render it "enriched," "prosper," or "made rich." The image is thriving, not literal body weight.

**Does this verse mean I should give even when money is tight?** Proverbs does not command reckless giving, and other passages urge providing for your own household (1 Timothy 5:8) and planning wisely (Proverbs 21:5). The verse commends a generous disposition, which can be practiced at any scale.

**Who wrote Proverbs 11?** It falls in the collection introduced as "The proverbs of Solomon" (Proverbs 10:1). The book as a whole gathers material from more than one source, including sayings attributed to Agur and Lemuel later on.
