**Short answer:** Psalm 37:5 says, "Commit thy way unto the LORD; trust also in him; and he shall bring it to pass" (KJV). The Hebrew behind "commit" pictures rolling a heavy load onto someone else. David is telling anxious believers to transfer the weight of their whole course of life to God, keep trusting, and let God accomplish what he has purposed — not to hand God a plan and expect it executed.

## The context: a psalm about not envying the wicked

Psalm 37 is a wisdom psalm attributed to David, structured as an acrostic through the Hebrew alphabet. Its subject is stated in verse 1: "Fret not thyself because of evildoers, neither be thou envious against the workers of iniquity" (KJV).

The problem it addresses is old and familiar: dishonest people appear to prosper while faithful people struggle. Verse 2 answers that they shall soon be cut down like grass. Then verses 3–7 give a series of commands that form one connected argument:

- **v. 3** — Trust in the LORD and do good.
- **v. 4** — Delight thyself also in the LORD, and he shall give thee the desires of thine heart.
- **v. 5** — Commit thy way unto the LORD; trust also in him; and he shall bring it to pass.
- **v. 6** — He shall bring forth thy righteousness as the light.
- **v. 7** — Rest in the LORD, and wait patiently for him.

Reading verse 5 inside that sequence changes it considerably. It is not primarily advice about achieving your goals. It is counsel for someone tempted to resentment because life looks unfair — and the specific "way" being committed is a life lived faithfully while others cut corners.

Verse 6 tells us what God will bring to pass: your righteousness brought forth as the light, your judgment as the noonday. Vindication, not necessarily success.

## What it means, phrase by phrase

**"Commit thy way unto the LORD."** The Hebrew verb *galal* means to roll. The image is a burden rolled off your own back onto another's. The same root appears in Proverbs 16:3 — "Commit thy works unto the LORD, and thy thoughts shall be established" (KJV) — and in Psalm 22:8.

The object is "thy way" — again the road metaphor for the direction and conduct of a life. Not a single request. The whole course.

**"Trust also in him."** Committing is a moment; trusting is a continuing state. The psalm pairs them because rolling a burden onto God and then quietly taking it back is the standard failure mode.

**"And he shall bring it to pass."** The Hebrew is compact — literally something like "and he will act" or "he himself will do it." That is worth noticing, because English "bring it to pass" can suggest God executes *your* plan. The Hebrew emphasizes that God will act. Verse 6 supplies the content of that action.

**Where faithful Christians differ:** how directly to read the psalm's promises about the prosperity of the righteous and the ruin of the wicked. Some read them as general wisdom principles about the ordinary shape of life, noting that Job and Ecclesiastes complicate any strict formula. Others emphasize eschatological fulfillment — the vindication comes ultimately, not necessarily now, which fits verses 9–11 and 37–38. Most interpreters hold both, and the psalm's own patience language ("wait patiently," v. 7) supports it.

## How to apply it today

Begin with the image. Rolling a load is a physical, deliberate act. It is not a mood or a hope. Committing your way to God means naming the specific weight — the career uncertainty, the strained relationship, the outcome you cannot control — and consciously transferring responsibility for it.

Then expect to do it repeatedly. "Trust also in him" is present tense in effect. Most people who have prayed this way know the experience of rolling the burden over in the morning and finding it back on their shoulders by afternoon.

Third, be honest about the psalm's actual comparison. Verse 1 assumes you are watching someone less scrupulous do better than you. That is a specific temptation, and Psalm 37's answer is not that you will win but that God will act and vindicate. If your reason for committing your way to God is that you expect him to deliver your preferred outcome, verse 6 will be a disappointment and verse 7 will be unbearable.

Finally, hold verse 5 with verse 4. The psalm assumes delight in God comes first. Desires shaped by delighting in him are the desires he grants.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What does "commit thy way" literally mean in Hebrew?**
The verb *galal* means to roll — the picture is rolling a heavy load off yourself and onto someone else. The same root appears in Proverbs 16:3 and Psalm 22:8. It describes a deliberate transfer of weight, not a vague feeling of releasing something.

**Does Psalm 37:5 promise God will make my plans succeed?**
Not as stated. The Hebrew phrase rendered "he shall bring it to pass" emphasizes that God himself will act, and verse 6 identifies what he brings about: your righteousness brought forth as the light, your justice as the noonday. The promise is vindication and God's faithful action, which may or may not match the outcome you had in mind.

**How does Psalm 37:5 relate to verse 4 about the desires of your heart?**
Verse 4 conditions the promise on delighting in the LORD, which shapes what a person desires in the first place. Verse 5 continues the same sequence — trust, delight, commit, rest — as one connected argument aimed at a believer tempted to envy the wicked. Reading verse 5 apart from that sequence tends to turn it into a formula for getting what you want.

**Why does the psalm keep telling readers to wait?**
Because the problem it addresses is the visible prosperity of dishonest people, which is not resolved quickly. Verse 7 says to rest in the LORD and wait patiently; verses 9–11 point to a future in which the meek inherit the earth. The psalm treats patience as essential rather than incidental to trusting God.
