**Short answer:** The Bible describes spiritual growth as something God produces and the believer participates in — never as self-improvement. Its images are agricultural and developmental: fruit that grows from a branch connected to a vine, infants who move from milk to solid food, and a good work God began that he promises to finish.

## Growth is commanded — and it is God's work

**2 Peter 3:18** gives the direct command: "But grow in grace, and in the knowledge of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ" (KJV). It is the letter's closing line, phrased as an instruction.

**Philippians 1:6** gives the other half: "Being confident of this very thing, that he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ" (KJV). The project is God's, and its completion is guaranteed.

**Philippians 2:12–13** holds both together — work out your own salvation, "For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure" (KJV). Scripture does not treat effort and grace as competitors.

## The mechanism: connection, not effort

**John 15:5** is Jesus's image: "He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing" (KJV). A branch does not strain to produce grapes; it stays attached.

**2 Corinthians 3:18** describes the process as gradual and gaze-shaped — believers beholding the Lord's glory "are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord" (KJV).

**Galatians 5:22–23** names the result as *fruit*, singular, with many facets: "love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, Meekness, temperance" (KJV). Fruit is grown, not manufactured.

## The pattern: milk before meat

**1 Peter 2:2** describes the appetite of new believers: "As newborn babes, desire the sincere milk of the word, that ye may grow thereby" (KJV).

**Hebrews 5:12–14** describes the failure to progress — readers who ought to be teachers still needing milk, while solid food belongs to the mature, "even those who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil" (KJV). The phrase "by reason of use" is the practical key: discernment develops through practice, not merely through information.

**Ephesians 4:14–15** adds the goal — no longer children tossed by every wind of doctrine, but "speaking the truth in love" and growing up into Christ.

## What growth looks like in practice

**Colossians 1:10** ties it to conduct: walking worthy of the Lord, "being fruitful in every good work, and increasing in the knowledge of God" (KJV).

**Romans 12:2** locates it in the mind — transformed "by the renewing of your mind" rather than conformed to the world.

**2 Peter 1:5–8** lists a progression from faith through virtue, knowledge, temperance, patience, godliness, brotherly kindness, and love — with the promise that these qualities, if they abound, keep a believer from being barren or unfruitful.

## Where Christians differ

Faithful Christians hold different views on how sanctification works. Some traditions emphasize a gradual, lifelong process; some teach a decisive later experience that transforms the believer's relationship to sin; others emphasize that believers are already positionally holy and grow into what is already true of them. These positions draw on real passages — Romans 6:1–14, Hebrews 10:14, 1 Thessalonians 5:23 — and read them in different orders. Christians also differ on how much progress to expect in this life, with Romans 7 read as either a mature believer's ongoing struggle or a description of life apart from the Spirit. What they hold in common is the conviction that God is at work and that the believer is called to respond.

## How to apply it today

Change the metric. If growth is fruit, then the question is not how much you know but what is actually appearing — patience where there was none, honesty where there was spin, generosity that costs something.

Attend to connection before output. John 15:5 puts abiding first for a reason. Practices like Scripture reading, prayer, and gathered worship are not achievements; they are how a branch stays attached.

Expect the milk-to-meat progression, and don't skip it. Hebrews 5:14 says discernment comes "by reason of use" — meaning ordinary repeated obedience in small matters is the training, not a preliminary to it.

And rest in Philippians 1:6 on the slow days. The promise is that the one who began the work finishes it. Growth that depends on your consistency alone was never what Scripture described.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What does it mean to grow in grace?**
2 Peter 3:18 pairs growing in grace with growing in the knowledge of Christ, so it means an increasing experience of and reliance on God's undeserved favor alongside deepening understanding of him. It is not becoming more self-sufficient. The command's placement at the end of Peter's letter frames it as the ongoing response to everything he has written.

**How long does spiritual growth take?**
Scripture consistently presents it as a lifelong process. Philippians 1:6 speaks of God completing his work "until the day of Jesus Christ," and 2 Corinthians 3:18 describes ongoing change "from glory to glory." Christians differ on how much progress to expect in this life, but the New Testament never treats maturity as a finished state before then.

**What is the difference between milk and meat in the Bible?**
Hebrews 5:12–14 uses milk for elementary teaching suited to new believers and solid food for teaching that requires maturity. The passage's own definition of maturity is practical: those who "by reason of use" have trained their senses to discern good and evil. The distinction is about developed discernment, not merely accumulated information.

**Can a Christian stop growing?**
Hebrews 5:12 rebukes readers who should have been teachers but still needed milk, so Scripture clearly treats stalled growth as a real possibility. 2 Peter 1:8–9 warns that lacking the listed qualities leaves someone barren and shortsighted. Christians differ on what stalled growth implies about a person's standing, but agree Scripture treats it as serious.
