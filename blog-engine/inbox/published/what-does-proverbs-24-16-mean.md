**Short answer:** Proverbs 24:16 says the mark of a righteous person is not that they never go down, but that they keep getting back up: "For a just man falleth seven times, and riseth up again: but the wicked shall fall into mischief" (KJV). The contrast is between someone God repeatedly restores and someone whose fall is final.

## The context: a warning against gloating

Proverbs 24:16 is easy to misread when lifted out on its own, because it is the *reason* clause for the verse before it. Verses 15–16 form a unit. Verse 15 warns the wicked not to lie in wait against the dwelling of the righteous or spoil his resting place. Verse 16 explains why that scheme is futile: knock this man down seven times and he will stand up again.

The following verses press the same theme from the opposite side: "Rejoice not when thine enemy falleth" (v. 17, KJV). So the passage is not primarily a motivational line about personal grit. It is about the durability God gives the righteous and the folly of celebrating anyone's collapse.

This section belongs to "the words of the wise" (Proverbs 24:23 introduces a further collection), a body of sayings gathered within the book's larger wisdom material.

## What it means, phrase by phrase

**"For a just man."** The Hebrew word is *tsaddiq* — the righteous person, one rightly related to God and living accordingly. Proverbs consistently contrasts this figure with the wicked.

**"Falleth seven times."** Seven in Scripture regularly signals fullness or completeness. The number is not a quota; it means repeatedly, thoroughly, again and again.

What is the falling? Interpreters differ, and both readings have support. Given the context of enemies lying in wait (v. 15), many read it as falling into *calamity* — disaster, loss, persecution. Others, noting Proverbs' broader moral vocabulary, include falling into *sin* and being restored through repentance. The Hebrew verb itself is general enough to cover both, and most commentators allow that the verse speaks to a life repeatedly knocked down, whether by circumstance or by failure.

**"And riseth up again."** This is the definitive clause. Rising is what distinguishes the righteous — and Scripture is clear the strength to rise is not self-generated. Psalm 37:24 says of the good man, "though he fall, he shall not be utterly cast down: for the LORD upholdeth him with his hand" (KJV).

**"But the wicked shall fall into mischief."** The contrast is not "the wicked fall more often." It's that their fall has no recovery. The word rendered "mischief" means calamity or ruin — they fall *into* it and stay.

## How to apply it today

This verse quietly redefines what a faithful life looks like. Many believers assume that repeated failure disqualifies them — that the genuinely righteous have a smooth line upward. Proverbs 24:16 says the righteous person's biography includes seven falls. The evidence of righteousness is the getting up.

That reframes discouragement. If you have failed at the same thing repeatedly — a besetting sin, a business, a relationship, your health — the number of falls is not the diagnostic. The direction after the fall is.

It also reframes how you treat other people's failures. Verse 17 forbids rejoicing when an enemy falls; verse 16 tells you not to write off a fallen brother or sister. Galatians 6:1 tells the spiritual to restore such a person in a spirit of meekness, "considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted" (KJV).

Practically, rising again usually looks unremarkable: confess honestly rather than minimize, receive God's forgiveness as actually given, take the next obedient step today rather than waiting to feel restored, and stay connected to people who will help you up. Ecclesiastes 4:10 notes the misery of the one who falls alone with no one to lift him.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does "falleth seven times" mean sin or misfortune?**
The passage's setting — enemies lying in wait against the righteous (v. 15) — points strongly toward calamity, and many read it that way. Others include moral failure, since Scripture elsewhere describes the righteous falling into sin and being restored (1 John 1:9). The Hebrew is broad enough that faithful interpreters land in both places, and the encouragement holds either way.

**Is seven a literal number?**
Almost certainly not. Seven regularly denotes completeness or fullness in Hebrew usage, so the sense is "however many times, repeatedly." It's a way of saying the falls are many and thorough, not a count you're allotted.

**Does this verse mean I'll always recover from everything?**
It describes God's sustaining of His people rather than guaranteeing that every specific loss is reversed. Psalm 37:24 supplies the ground: the LORD upholds him. Some losses in this life are not undone, and Scripture points ultimately to God's final restoration rather than to guaranteed earthly comebacks.

**How is this different from just "never give up"?**
The verse locates the power outside the person. Secular resilience says get up because you're strong; Proverbs sets the righteous against the wicked and Scripture attributes the rising to God upholding His people (Psalm 37:24; 145:14). The command is to rise; the capacity is given.
