**Short answer:** Proverbs 21:5 contrasts two ways of pursuing gain. Careful, sustained planning tends toward abundance; rushing tends toward lack. The proverb is not primarily about speed but about the difference between deliberate work and shortcut-seeking.

"The thoughts of the diligent tend only to plenteousness; but of every one that is hasty only to want" (KJV).

## The context

This verse belongs to the Solomonic collection running from Proverbs 10:1 through 22:16 — short, contrasting two-line sayings. Chapter 21 clusters several observations about wealth, justice, and the limits of human planning, and it is worth reading verse 5 alongside verse 31: "the horse is prepared against the day of battle: but safety is of the LORD" (KJV).

Those two verses together are the book's actual position on planning. Plan thoroughly; do not treat the plan as the guarantee. Proverbs holds both without embarrassment.

## Phrase by phrase

**"The thoughts of the diligent."** The Hebrew word rendered "thoughts" refers to plans, purposes, or devised schemes — deliberate mental work, not idle reflection. Diligence in Proverbs is a recurring character trait, contrasted throughout with the sluggard (see Proverbs 6:6–11, 10:4).

Notice that diligence here is located in *thinking*, not only in effort. The diligent person is not merely working hard; they are working on purpose.

**"Tend only to plenteousness."** The word "tend" is doing real work. This is a trajectory, not a guarantee. Proverbs describes how things generally run, and the same book elsewhere acknowledges injustice and reversal.

**"But of every one that is hasty."** The Hebrew describes someone pressing or rushing. In context this is the person chasing quick gain — the shortcut, the fast return, the plan that skips steps.

**"Only to want."** Lack. The proverb's claim is counterintuitive and worth sitting with: the person moving fastest toward money often ends up with less. Proverbs 28:22 says the same thing more bluntly, noting that the one hastening after riches does not consider that poverty will come upon him.

## Cross-references

- **Proverbs 28:20** — "he that maketh haste to be rich shall not be innocent" (KJV).
- **Proverbs 13:11** — wealth gotten by vanity shall diminish, but gathering by labor increases it.
- **Proverbs 6:6–8** — the ant stores in summer; provision made before it is needed.
- **Luke 14:28** — Jesus on counting the cost before building a tower.
- **Proverbs 21:31** — preparation is human work; safety belongs to the LORD.
- **James 4:13–15** — plans made without reference to God, corrected not by abandoning planning but by holding it loosely.

## How to apply it today

The most useful reading is not "go slower." It is "stop skipping steps." Haste in this proverb is not high tempo; it is impatience with process — the investment you did not research, the hire you did not check, the debt taken on to arrive somewhere faster.

Applied to money, this is the classic argument for steady accumulation over speculation. Proverbs 13:11 makes the pairing explicit: what is gathered gradually grows, what arrives suddenly tends to leave the same way.

Applied to work, it argues for planning as a spiritual discipline rather than a lack of faith. Some Christians treat detailed planning as distrust of God. Proverbs disagrees — the diligent person's *thoughts* are the thing being praised. Verse 31 supplies the balance: prepare the horse, and know the outcome is not yours to control.

A practical test: when you feel the pull to move fast on a financial or career decision, ask what specifically would be lost by waiting two weeks. If the answer is "the opportunity disappears," that urgency is itself information. Legitimate opportunities usually survive due diligence.

**Where faithful Christians differ:** how much planning is faithful. Some emphasize James 4:13–15 and warn against presumption in long-range planning. Others emphasize the Proverbs tradition and treat careful preparation as obedience. Most land in the same place practically — plan carefully, hold plans loosely, and say "if the Lord wills."

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Proverbs 21:5 saying it is wrong to move quickly?**
Not exactly. The contrast is between deliberate planning and impatient shortcut-seeking, not between fast and slow. Someone can act quickly on a well-considered plan. The proverb targets the person whose speed comes from skipping the work of thinking things through.

**Does this verse guarantee that hard planning makes you rich?**
No. Proverbs states general tendencies rather than promises, and the same chapter closes by noting that preparation is human work while safety belongs to the LORD (Proverbs 21:31). Diligence is presented as the wise path, not as a mechanism that always produces wealth.

**What does "hasty" mean in the Hebrew?**
The term describes pressing or rushing. In the wealth passages of Proverbs it consistently attaches to the person chasing quick gain — see Proverbs 28:20 and 28:22, which warn that hurrying after riches carries both moral and financial cost.

**Does the Bible support financial planning?**
Yes, extensively. Proverbs praises the ant that stores food before winter (6:6–8), commends gradual accumulation over sudden gain (13:11), and Jesus uses the example of counting the cost before building (Luke 14:28). James 4:13–15 adds the qualifier: plan, but without presuming on the future.
