**Short answer:** Isaiah 12:2 is a line from a victory song, declaring that because God Himself is the rescuer, fear no longer has the final say. "I will trust, and not be afraid" is a decision the singer makes about God's character, not a description of how he happens to feel.

## The context: a song at the end of the judgment

Isaiah chapters 7–12 form a unit sometimes called the Book of Immanuel. It runs through Assyrian threat, warnings of judgment, and then the great promise of a shoot from the stump of Jesse (Isaiah 11:1) who will rule in righteousness. Chapter 12 is the short hymn that caps the whole section — the response of a people who have come out the other side.

The chapter opens: "in that day thou shalt say, O LORD, I will praise thee" (v. 1, KJV). It is a future song given in advance, so that people still in the middle of the threat know what the ending sounds like. Verse 2 is its central affirmation.

Notably, the verse deliberately echoes the Song of the Sea, sung after the Red Sea crossing: "The LORD is my strength and song, and he is become my salvation" (Exodus 15:2, KJV). Isaiah is telling a later generation that the God of the Exodus is still the same God.

## What it means, phrase by phrase

The King James Version reads: **"Behold, God is my salvation; I will trust, and not be afraid: for the LORD JEHOVAH is my strength and my song; he also is become my salvation"** (Isaiah 12:2).

- **"Behold, God is my salvation"** — Not *God gives* salvation, but God *is* salvation. The Hebrew noun here (*yeshuah*) is the same root behind the name Jesus. Rescue is not a product God dispenses; it is who He is toward His people.
- **"I will trust, and not be afraid"** — A resolve, stated in the future tense. Trust here precedes the disappearance of fear rather than waiting for it. The order matters for anyone praying this verse while still frightened.
- **"the LORD JEHOVAH is my strength and my song"** — The KJV's doubled divine name reflects an emphatic Hebrew construction. God is both the *power* that carries you and the *content* of what you sing about — the strength and the subject.
- **"he also is become my salvation"** — Past tense. The song looks back on a completed rescue. Confidence about the future rests on something already done.

## Cross-references

- **Exodus 15:2** — The Song of the Sea, which this verse quotes almost directly.
- **Psalm 118:14** — The same line appears again: "The LORD is my strength and song."
- **Isaiah 26:3–4** — Perfect peace for the mind stayed on God, who is "everlasting strength" (KJV).
- **Psalm 27:1** — "The LORD is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear?" (KJV).
- **Habakkuk 3:18–19** — Rejoicing in the God of salvation even when the harvest fails.

## How to apply Isaiah 12:2 today

The structure of this verse is its most useful feature. It does not begin with "I feel safe." It begins with a fact about God, moves to a decision, and then grounds both in something God has already done. That is a sequence you can actually follow when afraid: name who God is, choose to trust, and remember a past rescue.

The song's original setting is worth keeping in view. This was given to people facing invasion — the verse is not a promise that trouble will bypass you. Isaiah 12 comes *after* chapters of judgment, not instead of them. The claim is that God is the rescuer on the far side of hard things.

Christians read the salvation language here at more than one depth. Some emphasize the immediate historical deliverance Isaiah's audience needed; others read it as pointing forward to the salvation accomplished in Christ, given the chapter's connection to the promised heir of Jesse in chapter 11. Both readings are widely held, and neither cancels the other.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why does the KJV say "the LORD JEHOVAH"?** The Hebrew has an emphatic doubling of the divine name, which the KJV translators rendered with two forms of it. Other translations simplify to "the LORD" or "the LORD GOD." Nothing theological hangs on the choice.

**Is Isaiah 12:2 quoting Exodus 15?** It closely echoes Exodus 15:2, and the parallel appears intentional. Isaiah frequently frames God's future deliverance as a new Exodus, so calling back to the Red Sea song ties the two rescues together.

**What does it mean that God "is" my salvation rather than gives it?** The phrasing makes rescue inseparable from God Himself rather than a benefit He hands out. Practically, it means the security described here cannot be lost the way a gift can, because it rests in God's own character.

**How can I "trust and not be afraid" if I'm still afraid?** The verse states a resolve rather than an achieved emotional state. Many of the psalms model the same order — declaring trust while the fear is still present (see Psalm 56:3). The choosing comes first; the settling often follows later.
