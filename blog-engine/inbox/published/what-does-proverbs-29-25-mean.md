**Short answer:** Proverbs 29:25 warns that letting other people's opinions control you is a trap, and that trusting God is the way out: "The fear of man bringeth a snare: but whoso putteth his trust in the LORD shall be safe" (KJV). The verse contrasts two objects of fear — people or God — and says only one of them leaves you free.

## The context: a chapter about who holds power

Proverbs 29 is part of the collection introduced in 25:1 as proverbs of Solomon "which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out" (KJV). The chapter deals heavily with authority, justice, and how rulers and the ruled behave — the righteous flourishing under good government (v. 2), the ruler who listens to lies (v. 12), the poor and the oppressor meeting together (v. 13).

Verse 25 lands in that setting, which sharpens it. In a world where powerful people can genuinely hurt you, the proverb doesn't say those people are harmless. It says fearing them is the thing that catches you.

## What it means, phrase by phrase

**"The fear of man."** This covers more ground than being afraid of someone. In Hebrew usage it includes dread of people, of their disapproval, of their power over your reputation, income, or standing. In modern terms it spans people-pleasing, conflict avoidance, and cowardice alike.

**"Bringeth a snare."** The image is a trap set for an animal — something that looks like open ground until it closes. That's precise. Fear of man rarely announces itself as fear; it presents as being polite, keeping the peace, being realistic, not making waves. You don't notice the snare until you find you can't say what you know to be true.

**"But whoso putteth his trust in the LORD."** The remedy is not a stronger personality. It's a transfer of reliance. Proverbs' consistent counterpoint to fearing man is fearing God — "the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (1:7, KJV) — because a greater fear displaces a lesser one.

**"Shall be safe."** The word carries the sense of being set on high, out of reach — like a fortress on a cliff. Compare Proverbs 18:10, where the name of the LORD is a strong tower and "the righteous runneth into it, and is safe" (KJV). This isn't a promise of no consequences; it's a promise about where your security is anchored.

## How to apply it today

Fear of man is one of the most common and least confessed sins, precisely because it wears socially acceptable clothes. Some diagnostics:

- You change your stated convictions depending on the room.
- You stay silent about something true because of what it would cost socially.
- You over-explain, over-apologize, or over-commit to keep someone from being displeased.
- A single critical comment reorganizes your whole day.
- You avoid a needed conversation and call it kindness.

The remedy the verse gives is not "care less." It's trust more — a specific redirection. Practically, that means asking before a hard moment: *whose approval am I actually seeking here?* And then, deliberately, resting your standing on God's verdict rather than the room's.

It also helps to distinguish fear of man from legitimate concerns. Scripture commends respect for authority (Romans 13:7), gentleness (Proverbs 15:1), and caution about genuine danger (Proverbs 22:3). Believers are also told to give an answer for their hope "with meekness and fear" (1 Peter 3:15, KJV). The difference is whether people's opinion is *informing* your judgment or *governing* it.

Faithful Christians will apply this differently in different settings — a believer under real persecution weighs speech and safety differently than one merely facing awkwardness. Proverbs gives the principle; wisdom applies it, and Jesus Himself sometimes withdrew from hostility rather than confronting it (John 8:59).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is all fear of people wrong?**
No. Scripture commends prudence about real danger (Proverbs 22:3) and honor toward legitimate authority (Romans 13:7). The snare is letting human approval or disapproval become the thing that governs your decisions and convictions — placing people where God belongs.

**How do I stop being a people-pleaser?**
The verse points to replacement rather than willpower: put your trust in the LORD. Practically that means grounding your identity in God's acceptance, naming specific moments where you bend the truth for approval, and starting with small acts of honest speech. Galatians 1:10 frames it as a question of whose servant you are.

**What does "shall be safe" promise?**
The Hebrew pictures being set securely on high, out of reach. It's a promise about ultimate security in God rather than immunity from earthly consequences — many faithful people in Scripture suffered real loss for speaking truth while remaining safe in this deeper sense.

**How is fearing God different from fearing man?**
Fear of God in Proverbs is reverent awe that produces wisdom and freedom (1:7; 9:10), while fear of man produces a trap. One frees you to act rightly because the highest authority is settled toward you; the other binds you to shifting human verdicts. Jesus contrasts them directly in Matthew 10:28.
