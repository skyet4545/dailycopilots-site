**Short answer:** In Acts 1:8 Jesus promises his disciples that the Holy Spirit will give them power — not for personal display, but to make them credible *witnesses* to what they had seen of his life, death, and resurrection — and he maps that witness outward in widening circles, from their own city to the ends of the earth.

The verse reads, "But ye shall receive power, after that the Holy Ghost is come upon you: and ye shall be witnesses unto me both in Jerusalem, and in all Judaea, and in Samaria, and unto the uttermost part of the earth" (Acts 1:8, KJV).

## The context

These are the last words Luke records Jesus speaking before the ascension (Acts 1:9). The disciples have just asked a very different question: "Lord, wilt thou at this time restore again the kingdom to Israel?" (1:6, KJV). They want a timetable for national restoration. Jesus declines to give them one — "It is not for you to know the times or the seasons" (1:7) — and redirects them from *when* to *what*: not a date to wait for, but a task to receive power for.

Luke wrote Acts as the second volume of a two-part work (see Acts 1:1, addressed like his Gospel to Theophilus). Acts 1:8 functions as the book's table of contents. The witness begins in Jerusalem (Acts 1–7), spreads through Judea and Samaria (Acts 8–12), and reaches out to the wider Roman world, ending with Paul preaching in Rome (Acts 28). The rest of the book is this verse happening.

## What it means, phrase by phrase

**"Ye shall receive power."** The Greek word behind "power" is *dynamis*. It is something given, not generated — the disciples are told to wait in the city until they are "endued with power from on high" (Luke 24:49, KJV). Notice the promise is not that they will be impressive, but that they will be enabled.

**"After that the Holy Ghost is come upon you."** The power is inseparable from the Person. Acts 2 narrates the fulfillment at Pentecost. Whatever else the Spirit does in Acts, Luke consistently connects his coming to bold speech about Jesus (Acts 4:31).

**"Ye shall be witnesses unto me."** A witness testifies to what he has seen. That is the qualification the apostles name when replacing Judas — someone who accompanied Jesus and can testify to the resurrection (Acts 1:21–22). The content of the witness is a person: *unto me*.

**"Jerusalem... Judaea... Samaria... the uttermost part of the earth."** These are concentric circles, and the third one is pointed. Samaritans were the neighbors most Judeans avoided (John 4:9). The mission is not allowed to stop at the boundary of the comfortable.

## Cross-references

- Matthew 28:18–20 — the parallel commission, with authority and the promise of Christ's presence.
- Luke 24:46–49 — repentance and remission of sins preached "among all nations," starting at Jerusalem.
- Acts 2:1–4 — the promise fulfilled at Pentecost.
- Acts 8:1–5 — persecution scatters believers, and the Judea-and-Samaria phase begins.
- Romans 1:16 — the same "to the Jew first, and also to the Greek" outward movement.

Christians differ on whether the *power* of Acts 1:8 arrives at conversion for every believer or in distinct later fillings, and whether the sign-gifts that accompany it in Acts continue today. Both readings appeal to Acts itself — the first to passages like Acts 2:38–39, the second to repeated fillings such as Acts 4:31. The promise of Spirit-enabled witness is common ground.

## How to apply it today

Start with the circle you are actually in. The disciples' first assignment was the city where Jesus had just been executed — the hardest room, not the easiest. Yours is your household, workplace, or campus. Second, remember what a witness owes: accuracy, not eloquence. You are not asked to argue people into belief but to say truthfully what you know of Christ. Third, let the geography stretch you. If your concern for the gospel stops precisely where your comfort does, Acts 1:8 is a direct challenge.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What kind of power does Acts 1:8 promise?**
The Greek *dynamis* here is enabling power tied to the Holy Spirit's coming, and in Acts it consistently shows up as courage and clarity in testifying about Jesus (Acts 4:8–13, 4:31). It is given for mission rather than for personal advantage. Luke never presents it as a reward for spiritual achievement.

**Is Acts 1:8 only for the apostles?**
The original hearers were the apostles, and their eyewitness role was unique — they had seen the risen Christ. But Acts shows the witness quickly carried by ordinary believers who were scattered by persecution (Acts 8:1–4) and by non-apostles like Philip and Stephen, so most Christians read the pattern as extending to the whole church even though the eyewitness credential does not.

**Why does Jesus mention Samaria specifically?**
Samaria named a real ethnic and religious rift; Judeans and Samaritans had centuries of hostility behind them (see John 4:9). By putting Samaria between "Judaea" and "the uttermost part of the earth," Jesus makes crossing that rift a required step, not an optional one.

**Did Jesus answer the disciples' question about restoring the kingdom?**
Not directly. He told them the timing belongs to the Father's authority (Acts 1:7) and gave them a commission instead. Interpreters differ on whether this postpones a future restoration of Israel or redefines the kingdom's advance as the spread of the gospel; the text itself simply declines to set a date.
