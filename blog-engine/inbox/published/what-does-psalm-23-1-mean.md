**Short answer:** Psalm 23:1 means that because God personally cares for you the way a shepherd cares for his sheep, you have everything you truly need. "I shall not want" is not a promise of luxury but of sufficiency — with God as your shepherd, you will never lack what is essential for your life and soul.

## The context

Psalm 23 is titled "A Psalm of David." Before David was a king, he was a shepherd boy in the fields of Bethlehem (1 Samuel 16:11). He knew firsthand what sheep need — food, water, protection, guidance — and how helpless they are without a shepherd. Rather than describing God from a distance, David draws on the most familiar work of his own hands and says: this is what God is to me.

The shepherd was also a royal image in the ancient Near East. Kings were called shepherds of their people, and God is repeatedly pictured as the Shepherd of Israel (Psalm 80:1). So David's opening line is both intimate and majestic.

## What it means, phrase by phrase

**"The Lord is my shepherd"** (KJV; the WEB reads "Yahweh is my shepherd"). Notice the word *my*. David does not say "a shepherd" or even "our shepherd" here — he claims a personal relationship. God is not a distant force but a caretaker who knows him by name.

**"I shall not want."** In older English, "want" means *to lack*. David is saying, "I will lack nothing I need." The rest of the psalm unpacks this: green pastures, still waters, restored soul, right paths, comfort in the valley, a prepared table. The point is not that the sheep never faces hardship — the psalm openly names "the valley of the shadow of death" — but that the sheep is never abandoned or under-provided.

## Cross-references

- **John 10:11** — Jesus says, "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep," directly applying the shepherd image to himself.
- **Psalm 100:3** — "We are his people, and the sheep of his pasture."
- **Isaiah 40:11** — "He will feed his flock like a shepherd."
- **1 Peter 2:25** — Christ is called "the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls."
- **Philippians 4:19** — "My God will supply every need of yours according to his riches in glory."

## How to apply it today

When you feel anxious about what you lack — money, security, answers, health — Psalm 23:1 invites you to reframe the question. The issue is not first "Do I have enough?" but "Who is my shepherd?" A sheep does not plan the route or find the pasture; it follows and trusts. You can name a specific worry today and consciously hand it to the Shepherd who has promised to provide.

It also reshapes contentment. "I shall not want" is a quiet declaration you can return to whenever comparison or fear creeps in. You are not on your own.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does "I shall not want" mean God will make me wealthy?**
No. The Hebrew idea is lacking nothing you genuinely need, not having everything you desire. The psalm promises provision, guidance, and God's presence — not material abundance. Reading it as a wealth guarantee misses David's point about trust and sufficiency.

**Who wrote Psalm 23?**
The psalm is titled "A Psalm of David." David was a shepherd in his youth before becoming king of Israel, which is why the shepherd imagery is so vivid and personal to him.

**How does Psalm 23:1 connect to Jesus?**
In John 10, Jesus calls himself "the good shepherd" who lays down his life for the sheep, and 1 Peter 2:25 calls him "the Shepherd of your souls." Christians read Psalm 23 as beautifully fulfilled in Jesus, who provides, guides, and protects his people.
