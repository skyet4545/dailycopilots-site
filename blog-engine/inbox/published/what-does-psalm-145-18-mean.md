**Short answer:** Psalm 145:18 promises that God draws near to anyone who genuinely turns to Him in prayer. "In truth" does not mean *flawlessly* or *with perfect faith* — it means sincerely, as opposed to going through religious motions with no intention behind them.

## The context: David's alphabet of praise

Psalm 145 is headed "David's Psalm of praise" and is an acrostic — each verse begins with a successive letter of the Hebrew alphabet, a structure that signals completeness, as if praising God from A to Z. It is the last psalm in the Psalter attributed to David, and it opens the great run of praise psalms that close the book.

Verse 18 sits in a section describing what God is *like* toward people in need (vv. 14–20): He upholds those who fall, opens His hand to the hungry, is righteous in all His ways, is near to those who call, fulfills the desire of those who fear Him, and preserves them. Nearness, in other words, appears in a list of God's ordinary habits — not His exceptions.

## What it means, phrase by phrase

The King James Version reads: **"The LORD is nigh unto all them that call upon him, to all that call upon him in truth"** (Psalm 145:18).

- **"The LORD is nigh"** — "Nigh" is older English for *near*. This is relational nearness, not a statement about geography; Scripture elsewhere affirms God is everywhere (Psalm 139:7–10). Here the point is attentive closeness, the difference between someone in the room and someone leaning in.
- **"unto all them that call upon him"** — Note the word *all*. The verse sets no boundary of nationality, status, or track record. "Calling upon" the LORD is the Old Testament's basic shorthand for prayer (Genesis 4:26).
- **"to all that call upon him in truth"** — The repeated phrase adds one qualifier. The Hebrew word behind "truth" (*emet*) carries the sense of reliability, faithfulness, and integrity. So the contrast is not sincere-and-skilled prayer versus clumsy prayer. It is real prayer versus performance — the kind of empty worship the prophets condemned when hearts were far from God (Isaiah 29:13).

That distinction matters pastorally. Someone praying badly, doubtfully, or with a shaky vocabulary is still praying in truth. Someone reciting words they have no intention of meaning is not.

## Cross-references

- **Psalm 34:18** — "The LORD is nigh unto them that are of a broken heart" (KJV).
- **Acts 17:27** — God "be not far from every one of us" (KJV).

## How to apply Psalm 145:18 today

The practical value of this verse is that it removes a barrier most people assume is there. If God's nearness depended on the quality of your prayers, everyone would be disqualified. It depends instead on whether you actually mean it.

So the useful question is not "was that a good prayer?" but "did I mean it?" One honest sentence qualifies. This is also a verse worth remembering when God feels distant — the psalm presents nearness as God's settled disposition, not a reward you earn back after a dry season. Christians differ on how much felt experience should be expected in prayer; some traditions emphasize awareness of God's presence, others emphasize trusting the promise regardless of feeling. Psalm 145:18 states the promise and leaves the feelings undiscussed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does "in truth" mean God ignores some prayers?** It distinguishes sincere prayer from empty ritual, which the prophets repeatedly rebuked. It is not a bar of eloquence or emotional intensity. The concern is honesty, not performance quality.

**Does Psalm 145:18 apply to non-Israelites?** The verse says "all them that call upon him," and the surrounding psalm speaks of God's goodness to all His works (v. 9). The New Testament picks this up explicitly in Romans 10:12–13, applying "whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord" to everyone.

**What if I don't feel God near when I pray?** The verse describes God's posture, not your emotions. Psalm 145 makes nearness part of God's character rather than a response to how prayer feels. Many faithful Christians go through long stretches where the promise is believed rather than felt.

**Is Psalm 145 really an acrostic?** Yes — its verses follow the Hebrew alphabet in order, though one letter is missing in the traditional Hebrew text. That structure is part of why the psalm reads like a comprehensive survey of God's character.
