**Short answer:** Psalm 16:8 describes a deliberate practice — David says he keeps the LORD constantly in view, like something set in front of him — and states the result: because God is beside him in the position of a defender, he will not be shaken. It is a verse about steadiness that comes from where your attention is fixed.

The verse reads, "I have set the LORD always before me: because he is at my right hand, I shall not be moved" (Psalm 16:8, KJV).

## The context

Psalm 16 carries the heading "Michtam of David" — a term whose exact meaning is uncertain, appearing over a small group of psalms. It opens with a plea, "Preserve me, O God: for in thee do I put my trust" (16:1, KJV), which suggests genuine danger rather than untroubled comfort.

The psalm then works through what David has chosen and refused. He rejects the worship of other gods and their offerings (16:4). He says the LORD is "the portion of mine inheritance and of my cup" (16:5, KJV) — inheritance language that recalls how the tribe of Levi received no land allotment because the LORD himself was their portion (Numbers 18:20). Verse 7 credits God with counsel; verse 8 states the resulting posture; verses 9–11 describe the effect: the heart glad, the flesh resting in hope, "the path of life" and "fulness of joy" in God's presence.

Peter quotes verses 8–11 in his Pentecost sermon (Acts 2:25–28), and Paul cites the same passage in Acts 13:35, both applying the psalm's confidence about not being abandoned to death to Christ's resurrection.

## What it means, phrase by phrase

**"I have set."** The verb is active. This is not a mood that arrived but a decision that was made and is maintained. Whatever David felt about his circumstances, he did something with his attention.

**"The LORD always before me."** "Before me" is spatial language for what is in one's field of vision. "Always" removes the compartment — not God in designated religious moments, but God as the constant background of ordinary awareness.

**"Because he is at my right hand."** In the ancient world, the right-hand position was where a defender, advocate, or supporter stood. The psalm's confidence is not that David is strong but that he is accompanied. Compare Psalm 121:5, "the LORD is thy shade upon thy right hand" (KJV).

**"I shall not be moved."** The Hebrew image is of not being made to totter or slip. Notice the claim is stability, not immunity. David is not saying nothing will come at him; he is saying it will not knock him over.

## Cross-references

- Psalm 16:1 — the psalm's opening plea for preservation, showing real danger in the background.
- Psalm 16:9–11 — the results: gladness, rest, the path of life, joy in God's presence.
- Acts 2:25–28 — Peter quotes this passage at Pentecost and applies it to Christ's resurrection.
- Acts 13:35–37 — Paul makes the same argument in Pisidian Antioch.
- Psalm 121:5–8 — the LORD as keeper at the right hand.
- Isaiah 41:10, 13 — God upholding with his right hand.
- Hebrews 12:2 — looking to Jesus, a New Testament parallel to fixed attention.

Christians agree that Peter reads Psalm 16 as pointing to Christ, but they describe the mechanics differently. Some hold that David spoke prophetically about the Messiah in the first place; others hold that the psalm describes David's own experience and is fulfilled in a fuller sense in Christ, whom the grave could not hold. Peter's own framing in Acts 2:29–31 — that David died and was buried, so the words must reach beyond him — is the anchor for both readings.

## How to apply it today

The verb is the practical part. "I have set" implies something you arrange rather than something you wait to feel. That has traditionally meant habits that put God in the field of view repeatedly through a day: fixed times of prayer, a verse kept somewhere visible, a practice of turning the first thirty seconds of a hard moment into a sentence addressed to God.

It also reframes what stability requires. David's confidence is stated as a consequence — *because* he is at my right hand — not as self-generated resolve. If your steadiness depends on your own strength, it fails when your strength does.

And note what the psalm does not promise. "I shall not be moved" is not "nothing will happen." Psalm 16 begins with a request for preservation, which means the writer is asking for help while making this claim. Both can be true at once.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What does it mean to set the LORD before you?**
It means deliberately keeping God in view rather than only remembering him at intervals. The phrase uses the language of what is placed in one's line of sight, and the verb is a choice David made and sustained. Practically, it points to habits that repeatedly return attention to God during ordinary activity.

**Why "at my right hand"?**
In the ancient world, the right-hand position was where a supporter, defender, or advocate stood — the place of the one who helps in a fight or a trial. Saying God is at his right hand means David is accompanied and defended, not that he is strong. Psalm 121:5 and Isaiah 41:13 use similar imagery.

**Does "I shall not be moved" mean nothing bad will happen?**
No. The psalm opens by asking God to preserve him (16:1), which implies real threat, and the Hebrew image is of not slipping or tottering rather than of being untouched. The promise is stability under pressure, not the absence of pressure.

**Why does Peter quote Psalm 16 in Acts 2?**
Peter cites verses 8–11 to argue that David's words about not being abandoned to the grave point beyond David himself, since David died and was buried (Acts 2:29–31). He applies them to Jesus's resurrection. Paul makes a parallel argument from the same psalm in Acts 13:35–37.
