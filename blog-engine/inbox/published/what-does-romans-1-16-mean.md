**Short answer:** Romans 1:16 is Paul's thesis statement. He says he is not ashamed of the gospel because it is not merely good advice but *God's own power* to rescue anyone who believes — and that "anyone" deliberately includes both Jews and Gentiles, which is the central argument of the whole letter.

The verse reads, "For I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ: for it is the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth; to the Jew first, and also to the Greek" (Romans 1:16, KJV).

## The context

Paul is writing to a church in Rome he has never visited (Romans 1:10–13). Romans 1:16–17 sits at the hinge between his personal introduction and the long theological argument that follows. Everything from Romans 1:18 through chapter 11 unpacks these two verses: what the gospel is, why it is needed, how it works by faith, and why it reaches Gentiles without erasing God's promises to Israel.

The setting sharpens the word "ashamed." Rome was the capital of an empire whose measure of worth was power, honor, and status. The message Paul carried centered on a Galilean executed by Roman crucifixion — a death so degrading it was not discussed in polite company. Paul admits elsewhere that this message looks like foolishness and scandal from the outside (1 Corinthians 1:23). Saying "I am not ashamed" in a letter addressed to Rome is not a throwaway line.

## What it means, phrase by phrase

**"I am not ashamed."** This is understatement making a strong claim, a common device in Paul. He is not offering a lukewarm endorsement; he is saying the gospel's apparent shame is outweighed by what it actually is.

**"The gospel of Christ."** *Euangelion* means announcement of good news — in the Roman world, the kind of proclamation made about an emperor's victory or accession. Paul has just defined its content in verses 1–4: God's Son, descended from David, declared Son of God by the resurrection.

**"It is the power of God unto salvation."** Not a description of power, but power itself — the same connection Paul makes in 1 Corinthians 1:18. The gospel does something when it is announced; it is the instrument God uses to save.

**"To every one that believeth."** The condition is faith, not ancestry, ritual, or moral track record. Paul will spend chapters 3 and 4 defending this, using Abraham as his case study (Romans 4:3).

**"To the Jew first, and also to the Greek."** "First" carries priority in sequence and in promise — the gospel came through Israel and was preached there first (see Acts 13:46) — but the "also" removes any barrier. Romans 2:9–11 repeats the same pairing to insist God shows no partiality in judgment either.

## Cross-references

- Romans 1:17 — the immediate follow-up: "The just shall live by faith."
- 1 Corinthians 1:18, 23–24 — the cross as foolishness to some and God's power to those being saved.
- Mark 8:38 — Jesus warns against being ashamed of him and his words.
- 2 Timothy 1:8, 12 — Paul urges Timothy not to be ashamed, and says again that he himself is not.
- Acts 13:46 — the gospel spoken "first" to Jews in Pisidian Antioch, then to Gentiles.

Faithful Christians read "to the Jew first" differently: some see a permanent priority and an ongoing role for Israel grounded in Romans 11:25–29, others see a historical sequence now completed in one unified people of God, appealing to Ephesians 2:14–16. Both positions take the phrase seriously; the verse itself simply asserts priority without defining its duration.

## How to apply it today

Notice what Paul does *not* say. He does not say he is not ashamed because the gospel is popular, reasonable-sounding, or socially useful. His reason is that it works — it is God's power to save. That reframes evangelistic nerves: the pressure is not on your delivery to make the message effective.

Practically, this means saying the actual content out loud rather than a vaguer, safer version of it. It also means expecting the message to land with people unlike you, since the verse's whole point is that its reach is not limited by category. And when the gospel does sound foolish in a given room, Romans 1:16 says that reaction was anticipated from the beginning.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why would Paul need to say he was not ashamed?**
The message centered on a crucified man, and crucifixion was a punishment designed to humiliate. In an honor-driven culture like Rome's, proclaiming a crucified Savior invited contempt (1 Corinthians 1:23). Paul names the shame directly and then explains why it does not deter him.

**What does "power of God unto salvation" mean?**
Paul is saying the gospel is the means God actually uses to save, not just information about salvation. The announcement itself is God's saving instrument when it meets faith. That is why Paul is so insistent throughout Romans that the message not be diluted.

**Does "to the Jew first" mean Jews are more important?**
Paul uses "first" for priority in order — the promises came through Israel and the message was preached there first — not for superior standing. He states plainly a chapter later that "there is no respect of persons with God" (Romans 2:11, KJV) and applies the same order to judgment as to salvation.

**How does Romans 1:16 relate to verse 17?**
Verse 17 gives the mechanism: in the gospel "the righteousness of God" is revealed "from faith to faith," concluding with the quotation from Habakkuk 2:4 that "the just shall live by faith" (KJV). Together the two verses set up the argument of the letter — the gospel saves, and it saves through faith.
