**Short answer:** In John 11:25 Jesus tells a grieving woman that resurrection is not merely a future event she has to wait for — it is a person standing in front of her. Death, for those who belong to Him, becomes an interruption rather than an ending.

## The context: four days too late

Jesus receives word that His friend Lazarus is sick, and deliberately waits two days before going to Bethany (John 11:6). By the time He arrives, Lazarus has been dead four days.

Martha meets Him on the road with a sentence that anyone who has grieved will recognize: "Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died" (v. 21, KJV). It is grief and faith in the same breath. Jesus tells her, "Thy brother shall rise again" (v. 23), and Martha gives the correct, orthodox answer — she knows he will rise at the last day (v. 24). Many Jews of the period held exactly that belief in a future general resurrection.

Verse 25 is Jesus' response to a right answer that is still too far away to help her today.

## What it means, phrase by phrase

The King James Version reads: **"Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live"** (John 11:25).

- **"I am"** — This is the fifth of the seven great "I am" statements in John's Gospel, alongside the bread of life, the light of the world, the good shepherd, and the way, the truth, and the life. Readers have long heard in this construction an echo of God's self-identification to Moses in Exodus 3:14.
- **"the resurrection"** — Jesus takes the doctrine Martha placed at the end of history and relocates it in Himself. She was thinking of a date; He points to a person.
- **"and the life"** — Not just the one who raises the dead, but the source of life itself. John has said this from the opening chapter: "in him was life" (John 1:4, KJV).
- **"he that believeth in me"** — The condition is trust, not achievement. In John's Gospel, believing is consistently the hinge.
- **"though he were dead, yet shall he live"** — Physical death is assumed, not denied. Believers do die. What Jesus denies is that dying is the end of the story.

Verse 26 completes the thought: "whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die. Believest thou this?" (KJV). Most interpreters read verse 25 as addressing physical death and verse 26 as addressing a deeper death that belief in Christ prevents outright. The chapter ends with Lazarus walking out of the tomb — a demonstration, not a proof of the whole claim, since Lazarus would die again.

## How to apply John 11:25 today

This is one of the most-read verses at funerals, and for good reason: it was first spoken at one. That setting is worth keeping. Jesus does not correct Martha's grief or tell her to stop crying — a few verses later He weeps Himself (John 11:35). The comfort here is not a rebuke of sorrow.

What the verse offers a grieving person is a change of address. Hope is not placed in a distant date on God's calendar but in someone present. Martha's problem was not bad theology; it was that her theology was too far away to hold. Jesus moved it into the room.

The verse also asks something. It ends, two lines later, with a question: "Believest thou this?" Christians differ in how much they emphasize the future bodily resurrection versus the present life Christ gives, and traditions vary in how they describe the state of the dead in the meantime. What is held in common across those differences is the claim in this verse — that the risen Christ is the ground of the hope, and that trusting Him is how a person receives it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does John 11:25 mean Christians won't die?** No. The verse explicitly assumes physical death — "though he were dead." What it denies is that death is final for those who trust Christ. Verse 26's "shall never die" is generally understood as referring to a deeper, spiritual death.

**Why did Jesus wait before going to Lazarus?** John 11:4 and 11:15 indicate the delay was purposeful, connected to what the disciples and others would come to believe. The text does not present it as indifference; Jesus is described as loving Martha, Mary, and Lazarus (v. 5).

**What's the difference between verse 25 and verse 26?** Verse 25 addresses those who die physically yet will live; verse 26 addresses those alive and believing who "shall never die." Most interpreters read the first as bodily resurrection and the second as immunity from spiritual death.

**Is Lazarus's raising the same as resurrection?** Not in the full sense. Lazarus was restored to ordinary mortal life and would die again, whereas Christian teaching about resurrection describes a permanent, transformed bodily life (1 Corinthians 15:42–44). The raising of Lazarus functions as a sign pointing toward it.
