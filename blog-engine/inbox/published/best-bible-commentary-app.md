If you want a Bible commentary app in 2026, the short answer depends on one question: do you need *classic* commentaries or *modern* ones?

- **Best free commentary app: Blue Letter Bible.** Every commentary in it is free, plus Strong's numbers, lexicons, and interlinear tools. The catch is that the library leans heavily on public-domain works from the 1700s and 1800s.
- **Best paid commentary library: Logos.** Nothing else comes close for modern, peer-reviewed commentary sets, and its subscription tiers start at $9.99/month.
- **Best middle ground: Olive Tree.** A genuinely free reader with a large à-la-carte commentary store, so you buy only the volumes you actually want.
- **Best for original languages on a one-time payment: Accordance.** Its $49.90 Basic Starter includes Greek and Hebrew texts without a recurring bill.
- **Best if you don't want a commentary at all, but an explanation:** an AI study app like Bible Copilot, which answers your specific question about a passage instead of handing you three pages on the whole chapter.

That last category is new, and it's worth naming plainly: an AI Bible app is not a commentary. It doesn't give you Calvin or Carson. It gives you a structured, Scripture-cited answer to what you actually asked. Some readers want both.

## Blue Letter Bible — the honest free winner

If your budget is zero, this is where to start. Blue Letter Bible bundles Strong's Concordance, interlinear Greek and Hebrew, lexicons, cross-references, and a stack of commentaries — all without a paywall or a premium tier waiting to ambush you.

The trade-off is real, though, and most "best of" lists skip it: the free commentary stack is largely 18th- and 19th-century. Matthew Henry and Jamieson-Fausset-Brown are excellent, but they were written before a century of archaeological and manuscript work. For devotional depth, that's fine. For a paper on the Synoptic problem, it isn't.

## Logos — the real answer for sermon prep

Logos is the research workstation of Bible software, and its commentary catalog is the reason people pay for it. As of 2026 it runs on subscription tiers: Premium at $9.99/month, Pro at $14.99/month, and Max at $19.99/month, with annual pricing at $99.99, $149.99, and $199.99 respectively, and a 30-day free trial. Existing Logos 10 owners and verified students get meaningful discounts.

Two honest caveats. First, the subscription model was contentious among longtime users who had already bought libraries outright — read the tier details carefully before committing. Second, Logos has a genuine learning curve. If you open it twice a year, you will not get $120 of value out of it.

## Olive Tree and Accordance — the middle of the market

Olive Tree's reader is free and good, and its business model is the store attached to it: study Bibles, commentaries, dictionaries, and atlases sold as individual purchases. That's the right shape if you want, say, the NIV Application Commentary on one book and nothing else.

Accordance appeals to a different instinct — one-time ownership. Registering an account unlocks a free iOS starter collection, and the $49.90 Basic Starter adds ESV, KJV with Strong's numbers, the Greek New Testament, and the Hebrew Bible. Its original-language handling has a long reputation among academics.

## Where an AI study app fits (and where it doesn't)

A commentary answers the questions its author decided to answer. An AI study app answers yours. When you're stuck on a clause in Romans at 10pm, "what does *propitiation* mean here and why does Paul use it?" is a faster route than scanning four pages of exposition.

Bible Copilot works this way. It runs six study modes — Summary, Observe, Interpret, Theology, Apply, and Apologetics — which mirror the inductive method rather than a chat free-for-all, and it cites the Scripture it's reasoning from. It's free for three questions a day with no account required, and you can [get it on the App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6758913373) if you're on iPhone. Pro is $9.99/month or $49.99/year with a 7-day trial.

Be clear on the limits. It's iOS-only. It does not ship Calvin, Keller, or the Word Biblical Commentary. And like any AI, it can be confidently wrong — which is exactly why the Scripture citations matter: you can check it. For serious exegetical work, use it alongside a real commentary, not instead of one.

## How to choose in 30 seconds

- **You have no budget:** Blue Letter Bible.
- **You preach or teach weekly:** Logos, and take the trial first.
- **You want one or two specific commentaries:** Olive Tree.
- **You hate subscriptions:** Accordance.
- **You mostly need passages explained, not surveyed:** an AI study app, with a commentary as backup.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the best free Bible commentary app?**
Blue Letter Bible. Its commentaries, lexicons, and interlinear tools are free with no premium tier. Bible Hub and StudyLight.org offer even larger free commentary collections, though both are strongest on the web rather than as native apps.

**Is Logos worth the subscription cost?**
It is if you prepare sermons, lessons, or academic work regularly — the modern commentary sets and search tools genuinely save hours. It is not if you study occasionally; Blue Letter Bible covers that use case for free. Take the 30-day trial before deciding.

**Can an AI Bible app replace a commentary?**
No. Commentaries carry an author's accountability, scholarly apparatus, and denominational context that an AI answer doesn't. AI is better at answering a specific question quickly; a commentary is better at sustained, verifiable exposition. Use AI to get oriented, then check it against a commentary and the text itself.

**Are old public-domain commentaries still reliable?**
Often yes for theology and devotional insight, less so for history and language. Works like Matthew Henry's predate significant manuscript discoveries and archaeological findings. Pair them with something modern when the historical background actually matters to your question.

**Which commentary app works offline?**
Olive Tree, Accordance, e-Sword, and Logos all support downloading resources for offline use — useful for travel or church buildings with poor signal. Web-first tools like Bible Hub and StudyLight require a connection, and AI study apps do too, since the answers are generated on request.

## A closing note

No app makes the text speak for you. The tools above shorten the distance between a question and a credible answer, and that's worth something — but the reading is still yours to do. As the Psalmist put it in the World English Bible, "Your word is a lamp to my feet, and a light for my path" (Psalm 119:105). The apps just help you hold the lamp steady.
