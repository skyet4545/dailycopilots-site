**Short answer:** Isaiah 55:11 promises that what God speaks reliably produces what he sent it to produce. The surrounding image is rain and snow — they do not evaporate uselessly; they water the ground and yield a harvest. The claim is about God's effectiveness, not about human speech.

"So shall my word be that goeth forth out of my mouth: it shall not return unto me void, but it shall accomplish that which I please, and it shall prosper in the thing whereto I sent it" (KJV).

## The context

Isaiah 55 closes a long section of the book addressed to a people in exile. The chapter opens with an invitation — everyone who thirsts, come to the waters — and offers food and drink without money. It calls the wicked to forsake their way and return, promising abundant pardon (verse 7).

Then verses 8–9 make the famous statement that God's thoughts are not our thoughts and his ways are higher than ours. That is often quoted as a general note about divine mystery, but in context it is specifically about *mercy* — God forgives on a scale that does not match human instinct.

Verses 10–11 form a single sentence built on a comparison:

Verse 10 — rain and snow come down from heaven and do not return there, but water the earth, making it bring forth and bud, giving seed to the sower and bread to the eater.

Verse 11 — "so shall my word be."

The whole unit is therefore about the certainty of God's promise to restore. The word that will not return void is, first of all, the word of pardon and return just offered.

## Phrase by phrase

**"My word that goeth forth out of my mouth."** God's spoken purpose — what he declares he will do. In Isaiah this repeatedly refers to prophetic announcement, especially the promise of restoration.

**"It shall not return unto me void."** Empty-handed. The image is of a messenger sent on an errand who comes back without having accomplished it. God's word does not do that.

**"But it shall accomplish that which I please."** The measure of success is God's intent, not the observer's expectation. This is a crucial qualifier — the word accomplishes *what God sent it to do*, which may not be what a reader assumes it should do.

**"And it shall prosper in the thing whereto I sent it."** The errand is completed. Sent for a purpose, and the purpose is achieved.

## Cross-references

- **Isaiah 46:10** — God declares the end from the beginning; "my counsel shall stand" (KJV).
- **Isaiah 6:9–10** — a commission where God's word hardens rather than converts, showing that "what I please" is not always a warm outcome.
- **Jeremiah 23:29** — God's word is like fire and like a hammer that breaks rock.
- **Hebrews 4:12** — the word of God is living and powerful, sharper than a two-edged sword.
- **Matthew 13:3–23** — the parable of the sower, where the same seed meets four different soils.
- **1 Thessalonians 2:13** — the word "effectually worketh" in those who believe (KJV).

## How to apply it today

This verse is frequently used to claim that any Scripture spoken over a situation will produce a specific desired result. That reading has to ignore the qualifier in the verse itself: the word accomplishes what *God* pleases. It is a promise about God's purposes succeeding, not a mechanism for securing ours.

Read properly, it is more comforting, not less. It means the Bible's promises are not aspirational. What God has said about redemption, judgment, and restoration is going to happen — regardless of whether current evidence supports it. Isaiah's original readers were in exile, looking at a demolished city, being told that God's word of return would stand. Nothing they could see agreed.

Practically, this gives a reason to keep speaking, teaching, and reading Scripture when it appears to accomplish nothing. Isaiah's own commission in chapter 6 involved decades of apparent failure. The parable of the sower assumes most seed will not produce. Neither passage treats visible results as the measure.

It also cautions against measuring a sermon, a conversation, or a season of reading by its immediate effect. The errand is God's, and so is the timeline.

**Where faithful Christians differ:** how much this verse supports "declaring" Scripture to change circumstances. Charismatic traditions often emphasize the active power of the spoken word; Reformed and other traditions emphasize God's sovereign purpose and warn against treating the verse as a formula. Both affirm that God's word is effective; they differ on who is doing the sending.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does Isaiah 55:11 mean anything I pray from Scripture will come true?**
The verse promises that God's word accomplishes what *God* sends it to do — the qualifier is in the text. It is a statement about the reliability of God's purposes rather than a guarantee that a verse applied to a personal situation will produce the outcome requested.

**What is the rain-and-snow comparison in verse 10 for?**
Verses 10 and 11 form one sentence. Rain and snow do not evaporate uselessly; they soak the ground and produce seed and bread. God's word works the same way: it is sent, it does its work, it yields something. The image emphasizes productivity, not speed.

**Can God's word "fail" if people reject it?**
Isaiah 6:9–10 and the parable of the sower in Matthew 13 both describe God's word meeting rejection, yet neither treats it as failure — in Isaiah's commission, hardening is part of what the word was sent to do. Since the standard is God's intent, apparent rejection does not mean the word returned empty.

**What was the "word" Isaiah's first readers were meant to trust?**
In context, the promise of pardon and restoration offered earlier in the chapter — the invitation to return, and God's abundant mercy in verses 6–7. The audience was in exile with no visible evidence supporting that promise, which is exactly why the assurance was given.
