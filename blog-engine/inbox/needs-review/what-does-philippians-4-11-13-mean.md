**Short answer:** Philippians 4:11-13 is Paul explaining that he has learned contentment in every circumstance — poverty and plenty, hunger and abundance — and that verse 13's famous line, "I can do all things through him who strengthens me," is specifically about the strength to endure both hardship and abundance well, not a general promise of achievement or success.

## The context: thanking the Philippians without sounding needy

Paul writes this passage while thanking the Philippian church for a financial gift they sent him (see 4:14-18, "you have sent me help for my needs"). Because he doesn't want the thanks to sound like a complaint about being in need before their gift arrived, Paul first clarifies his own posture toward material circumstances in 4:11-13, then thanks them directly afterward. The verses function as a disclaimer: he's grateful for their gift, but his contentment was never dependent on receiving it.

## What it means, phrase by phrase

The ESV reads: "Not that I am speaking of being in need, for I have learned, in whatever situation I am, to be content. I know how to be brought low, and I know how to abound. In any and every circumstance, I have learned the secret of facing plenty and hunger, abundance and need. I can do all things through him who strengthens me."

- **"I have learned...to be content"** — the word "learned" matters. Paul does not present contentment as a natural personality trait or an instant gift; it is something acquired over time, through experience of both directions (want and abundance).
- **"I know how to be brought low, and I know how to abound"** — Paul had genuinely experienced both extremes: imprisonment, hunger, and need (see 2 Corinthians 11:27), and also seasons of provision and support. His contentment claim is not theoretical.
- **"The secret"** — the Greek word here (memyēmai) was used in the mystery religions of the time for being "initiated" into hidden knowledge; Paul repurposes the term to describe contentment as something learned through lived experience rather than abstract instruction.
- **"I can do all things through him who strengthens me"** — read in isolation, this verse is often applied to athletic performance, exams, or general achievement. In context, "all things" refers specifically back to the "any and every circumstance" of verse 12 — facing plenty and hunger, abundance and need. The strength Paul describes is the strength to remain content and stable through both hardship and prosperity, not a promise that faith guarantees success at any undertaking.

## Cross-references

- **2 Corinthians 12:9-10** — Paul's related teaching that Christ's power is "made perfect in weakness," another passage where Paul finds strength specifically in difficult circumstances rather than despite them.
- **1 Timothy 6:6-8** — "godliness with contentment is great gain... if we have food and clothing, with these we will be content," a parallel teaching on contentment from one of Paul's other letters.
- **Hebrews 13:5** — "keep your life free from love of money, and be content with what you have," another New Testament call to the same virtue.

## How to apply it today

The most common misreading of this passage lifts verse 13 out of its "plenty and hunger, abundance and need" context and turns it into a general-purpose promise of success — a mindset in which any goal becomes achievable through faith. Paul's actual point is narrower and, in some ways, more demanding: the strength he's describing is the strength to stay stable and content specifically when circumstances swing between scarcity and abundance, not a formula for guaranteed outcomes in whatever a person attempts. Read together, verses 11-13 describe contentment as a learned discipline built through both kinds of experience, with Christ's strength as the sustaining power behind that stability rather than a guarantee of getting what one wants.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does Philippians 4:13 mean I can accomplish anything if I have enough faith?**
Not in its original context. "All things" in verse 13 refers back to the specific circumstances named in verses 11-12 — facing both plenty and hunger, abundance and need — not an open-ended promise about achievement, career success, or any goal a person sets.

**Is contentment in this passage the same as not caring about outcomes?**
No — Paul actively thanks the Philippians for their financial gift in the verses immediately following (4:14-18) and clearly valued their support. Contentment here means his stability and identity were not dependent on his circumstances, not that circumstances didn't matter to him at all.

**Why does Paul call contentment a "secret" he learned?**
The Greek term evokes the language of initiation into hidden knowledge, which Paul repurposes to describe contentment as something acquired through lived experience of both hardship and abundance, not a trait some people are simply born with.
