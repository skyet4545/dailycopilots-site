## What Happened in 2 Samuel 11

While his army was at war, King David stayed behind in Jerusalem. From his roof he saw Bathsheba bathing and sent for her, though he knew she was married to Uriah the Hittite, one of his own soldiers. She became pregnant. To hide it, David brought Uriah home from battle hoping he would sleep with his wife — but Uriah, out of loyalty to the men still fighting, refused to enjoy comfort while they camped in the open field. So David sent Uriah back to the front carrying his own death sentence: a letter ordering his commander Joab to place Uriah where the fighting was fiercest, then withdraw support so he would be killed. Uriah died. David married Bathsheba.

## Nathan's Parable (2 Samuel 12)

God sent the prophet Nathan to confront David. Nathan told a story about a rich man with many flocks who, rather than take from his own, stole a poor man's only lamb — "which he had bought and reared, and which had grown up with him and with his children" — to feed a guest. Furious, David condemned the rich man to death. Nathan answered: "You are the man!" (2 Samuel 12:7, ESV). Nathan then pronounced God's judgment: the sword would never leave David's house, and the child born of the affair would die.

## Repentance and Consequence

David did not deny it. His response — "I have sinned against the LORD" (2 Samuel 12:13) — is echoed in Psalm 51, traditionally understood as his prayer of repentance: "Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me" (Psalm 51:10). Nathan told him the LORD had "put away" his sin, but the consequences remained: the child died, and turmoil followed David's household for years afterward.

## Why the Story Still Matters

David was not a footnote figure — he was Israel's most celebrated king, described elsewhere as "a man after [God's] own heart" (1 Samuel 13:14). Scripture doesn't soften his failure to protect his reputation. It records the sin plainly and records the repentance just as plainly, showing that neither status nor past faithfulness exempts anyone from accountability — and that honest confession, not self-justification, is what restoration requires.
