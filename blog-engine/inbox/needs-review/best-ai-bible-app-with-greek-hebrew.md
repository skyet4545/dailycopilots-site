Short answer, for a reader who doesn't know the alphabets and doesn't want to learn them: **use an AI app to *explain* the original-language word, and a free lexicon tool to *verify* it.** No single app does both well yet.

For plain-English explanation of what a Greek or Hebrew word carries — inside an actual study of the passage — [Bible Copilot](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6758913373) is the pick on iOS, because its Interpret mode surfaces the underlying term and cites the verses it's reasoning from. For verification, **Blue Letter Bible** is the best free tool in existence: tap a word, get the Strong's number, the lexicon entry, and every other place it appears. **STEP Bible**, free from Tyndale House in Cambridge, is the scholar-grade sibling worth knowing about. And if you want AI and interlinear text literally side by side in one app, **Interlinear Bible: Strong's** is the newest entrant doing exactly that.

If you have money and ambition, **Logos** is the only tool here that is genuinely strong at both: real morphological tagging *and* an AI search layer. It's also the most expensive.

One honest warning up front, which the rest of this article unpacks: **general-purpose AI is unreliable on original-language specifics.** It is good at concepts and bad at parsing.

## What AI actually does well here — and what it doesn't

The useful distinction isn't "AI vs. traditional." It's **explanation vs. verification.**

AI is genuinely good at explanation. Ask why John's Gospel opens by calling Jesus *logos*, and a good model will walk you through the Greek philosophical background, the Hebrew wisdom tradition behind it, and why English "Word" is a thin translation. That's real teaching, and it's the part most readers were missing.

> "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." (John 1:1, World English Bible)

AI is unreliable at verification. Ask it to parse a verb, give you a Strong's number, or tell you how many times a lemma appears in Luke, and error rates climb fast — often delivered with total confidence. Strong's numbers in particular are exactly the kind of arbitrary identifier language models reconstruct badly. Treat any specific number, count, or parsing from a chatbot as a claim to check, not a fact.

So the workflow that actually works for a non-specialist is two apps: one that teaches you what to ask, one that lets you confirm the answer.

## The picks

### Bible Copilot — best for understanding the word in context
Bible Copilot is an AI Bible study app for iOS built around six modes: Summary, Observe, Interpret, Theology, Apply, and Apologetics. The Interpret mode is where original-language material shows up — it names the Greek or Hebrew term behind a key English word and explains the range of meaning in plain language, with the Scripture it's drawing on cited so you can check the reasoning against the text.

Be clear about what it is not. It has no interlinear, no lexicon, no morphology search, and no Strong's lookup. It's iOS-only. If you want to search every occurrence of a lemma, this is the wrong tool. If you keep hitting words where the English feels flattened — *hesed*, *sarx*, *dikaiosune* — and you want to know what's underneath without a seminary course, this is the fastest path. Free tier is 3 questions a day with no account required; Pro is $9.99/mo or $49.99/yr with a 7-day trial.

### Blue Letter Bible — best free verification layer
Nothing else free comes close. Every verse links to an interlinear, Strong's numbers, lexicon entries, and concordance results. It has no AI and doesn't need one — its job is to be right, and it is. Pair it with any AI app and you've covered both halves. If you install one thing from this article, install this.

### STEP Bible — best free scholar-grade tool
Built by Tyndale House in Cambridge and donation-supported, STEP Bible lets you search by lemma, morphology, and root across the Tyndale House Greek New Testament, a morphologically tagged Hebrew Bible, the Septuagint, the Latin Vulgate, and dozens of English versions — simultaneously. It's a web app and it's less friendly than Blue Letter Bible, but for grammatical searching it's remarkable for free.

### Logos — best if you're paying anyway
Logos is the only entry strong on both sides. Its reverse interlinear maps your English translation back to the underlying Hebrew or Greek word by word with morphological tags on every term, and Smart Search answers natural-language questions from your own library — AI grounded in real books rather than model memory. Subscriptions run $9.99/mo (Premium), $14.99/mo (Pro), or $19.99/mo (Max) after a 30-day trial, and many commentary sets are still sold separately, often at $49 and up. There's no permanent free tier. It's the best tool here and the one most people don't need.

### Interlinear Bible: Strong's — the interesting new hybrid
This one puts the full Hebrew and Greek text, Strong's numbers, and word-by-word English alignment in the same app as an AI chat assistant — the combination this category has been missing. It's newer and less proven than the established names, and its pricing isn't clearly advertised outside the App Store listing, so check that before committing.

### Faith Guide and Bible Chat — good apps, wrong category
Both are legitimate AI Bible apps. Faith Guide is free with unlimited conversations and keeps a careful pastoral tone — reviewers note it leans toward encouraging personal discernment when Scripture doesn't speak plainly, which some readers will want and others will find too soft. Bible Chat is a polished devotional companion with audio Bible, plans, and prayer. Neither offers original-language tooling, so if Greek and Hebrew are your reason for downloading, neither is your answer.

### ChatGPT and Claude — fine for thinking, not for checking
General assistants reason well about biblical languages conceptually, but have no binding to a lexicon database. Specific parsings, occurrence counts, and Strong's numbers should always be confirmed elsewhere.

## How to choose

- **You want to understand a word, not catalogue it:** an AI study app — Bible Copilot on iOS — plus Blue Letter Bible to check it.
- **You want everything free:** Blue Letter Bible plus STEP Bible covers more ground than most paid setups from ten years ago.
- **You want grammatical search:** STEP Bible free, Accordance or Logos paid.
- **You already own a Logos library:** stay there. Smart Search over books you own is the most trustworthy AI in this space.

Denominational traditions differ on how much weight original-language argument should carry, and honest scholars disagree about individual words. Any tool — AI or lexicon — is a starting point for study, not a verdict.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can AI accurately translate Greek and Hebrew?**
For general sense, usually yes. For precise parsing, Strong's numbers, or occurrence counts, no — error rates are meaningful and the errors arrive sounding confident. Use AI to understand a word's range of meaning, then confirm specifics in Blue Letter Bible or STEP Bible.

**What is the best free AI Bible app with Greek and Hebrew?**
There isn't one app that's both free and strong at both. The best free combination is Blue Letter Bible or STEP Bible for the language data, paired with a free AI tier — Bible Copilot allows 3 questions a day without an account, and Faith Guide is free for unlimited chat but has no original-language tools.

**Do I need to learn Greek or Hebrew to benefit from these tools?**
No. Strong's numbers exist precisely so English readers can trace a word back to its original without reading the alphabet. Interlinears align the original text under the English. You'll get real value from an afternoon of use — though tools can't replace grammar knowledge when a passage genuinely hinges on syntax.

**Is Logos worth it just for original languages?**
For most readers, no. Blue Letter Bible and STEP Bible deliver the core original-language capability for free. Logos earns its price through library depth, morphological searching, and AI grounded in resources you own — which matters for pastors, teachers, and students, and rarely for a daily reader.

**Why do AI Bible apps disagree about what a word means?**
Because lexicons and scholars sometimes disagree, and because models weigh sources differently. Words carry a range of meaning shaped by context, not a single fixed definition. When two tools differ, the answer is to look at how the word is actually used elsewhere in Scripture — which is what the concordance in a free tool like Blue Letter Bible is for.
