What is the Shema in the Torah?

If you want the shortest answer: the Shema is the covenant declaration in Deuteronomy 6 that joins God’s oneness, wholehearted love, daily recitation, and transmission to the next generation.

The Shema begins with the line from Deuteronomy 6:4: “Hear, O Israel: the LORD is our God, the LORD is one.” The opening word Shema means hear or listen, but the force of the passage is larger than hearing sounds. It calls Israel to attentive, loyal response.

The next verses explain why the passage is central. They command love of God with the whole heart, soul, and might; they tell Israel to place these words on the heart, teach them diligently to children, speak them at home and on the way, bind them on the hand and between the eyes, and write them on the doorposts. That is why the passage becomes linked to daily recitation, tefillin, and mezuzah.

The Shema is also a good example of why Jewish study distinguishes Written Torah from Oral Torah. The written verses name the commands directly, but questions about how they are recited and practiced are exactly the kind of questions the interpretive tradition takes up in detail.

Quick guide

Opening words
“Hear, O Israel: the LORD is our God, the LORD is one.”
What Shema means
Hear or listen, with the sense of attentive covenant response.
Main themes
God’s oneness, total love of God, memory, teaching, and daily practice.
Why it matters here
It shows how one short Torah passage opens into a larger life of study and observance.

Source map: declaration, command, and practice

Declaration
Deuteronomy 6:4
The opening line names God’s oneness and gives the passage its title: Shema.
Covenant response
Deuteronomy 6:5
The next verse commands wholehearted love of God, showing that the declaration is meant to lead into loyalty and action.
Transmission
Deuteronomy 6:6-7
The words are to be placed on the heart and taught diligently to children, making memory and instruction part of the passage itself.
Embodied signs
Deuteronomy 6:8-9
The commands to bind these words and write them on the doorposts become the basis for tefillin and mezuzah in later Jewish practice.
