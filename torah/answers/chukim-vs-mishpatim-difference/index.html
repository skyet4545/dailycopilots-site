What is the difference between chukim and mishpatim?

If you want the shortest answer: mishpatim are commandments whose reason seems plain to human judgment, while chukim are commandments obeyed first as divine decrees even when their reason is not obvious.

Mishpatim are the laws most people can recognize as intelligible ethical or civil norms: do not steal, do not murder, return what is lost, judge fairly. Chukim are the laws whose rationale is not self-evident and which Jewish tradition treats as a test of obedience before explanation.

The classic example of a chok is the red heifer ritual in Numbers 19. It is not only complex but paradoxical: the ritual purifies the impure while making the administering priest impure. That is exactly the kind of command later Jewish tradition points to when it asks whether a mitzvah must be rationally transparent before it can be meaningful.

That is why the category matters. It is not only a filing system for commandments. It is a way of asking how Torah study holds together divine authority, human understanding, and the desire to know why a mitzvah was given.

Quick comparison

Whether obedience requires explanation first, or whether understanding can follow practice.

Source map: Torah text and later interpretation

Torah command
Leviticus 18:4
Leviticus commands Israel to keep God’s statutes and ordinances, giving the tradition the pair of terms it later explores in more detail.
Classic paradox
Numbers 19
The red heifer ritual becomes the paradigmatic chok because its purification pattern is difficult to reduce to ordinary moral logic.
Rashi
Obey first
Rashi emphasizes that some decrees are mocked as irrational and are still to be obeyed because they come from God.
Rambam
Reason may exist
Rambam argues that every mitzvah has a purpose even if its rationale is not obvious to us, so chukim are not reasonless simply because their reasons are hidden.

What this category is for

This distinction is not mainly about separating “easy laws” from “weird laws.” It is about how covenant obedience works. Some commandments appeal quickly to common moral sense. Others ask for loyalty before clarity.
