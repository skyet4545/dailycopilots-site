What is Cleanthes' Hymn to Zeus?

Cleanthes (c. 330-c. 230 BC), the second head of the Stoic school after Zeno, wrote a 39-line hymn to Zeus that survives complete, quoted in full by the later anthologist John Stobaeus - making it the earliest fully intact Stoic text we possess, older than anything from Epictetus, Seneca, or Marcus Aurelius. It opens: "Most glorious of immortals, many-named, always all-powerful, Zeus, first cause of nature, who rules all things with law, greetings to you." Rather than addressing Zeus as a mythological figure with a personality and a family tree, Cleanthes identifies him with the rational, ordering principle - the logos - that structures the entire cosmos: "for this whole cosmos, spinning around the earth, obeys you, wherever you lead, and is willingly ruled by you." This is a distinctly Stoic move, folding traditional Greek religious language into Stoic physics: Zeus is not a being who intervenes arbitrarily but is effectively identical with the rational law of nature itself, the same universal reason that later Stoics urge individuals to align their own reason with. The hymn also addresses the problem of apparent evil and wrongdoing in the world, arguing that even what looks disordered or bad from a limited human vantage point is woven by Zeus's universal reason into a coherent whole, "so that all things are brought into one and the same account, good and bad," a single unified order that particular human judgments simply cannot see in full. The hymn closes with a plea for wisdom: it asks Zeus to free humanity from ignorance so people might honor him "with justice," tying right worship directly to right understanding of nature and virtuous conduct rather than to ritual observance alone - a fitting opening statement for a school that treated theology, physics, and ethics as one interconnected system rather than three separate subjects.
Source: Cleanthes, Hymn to Zeus (preserved in Stobaeus, Anthology 1.1.12)
