What is the 'view from above' in Marcus Aurelius's Meditations?

In Meditations 7.48, Marcus Aurelius describes a mental exercise later named by scholar Pierre Hadot the "view from above": "One who would converse about human beings should look on all things earthly as though from some point far above — upon herds, armies, and agriculture, marriages and divorces, births and deaths, the clamor of law courts, deserted wastes, alien peoples of every kind, festivals, lamentations, and markets." The instruction is to deliberately imagine viewing ordinary life — weddings, wars, harvests, funerals, court disputes — from a vast height, the way one might look down on an anthill, so that everything appears as part of a single interwoven pattern rather than as isolated events demanding an anxious, close-up reaction. Marcus returns to variations of this exercise throughout the Meditations, elsewhere imagining human generations rising and falling "like waves" or comparing entire empires to specks. The purpose is not detachment for its own sake but perspective correction: a problem that consumes someone entirely at ground level — a public insult, a career setback, a rival's success — tends to shrink into scale once set beside the vastness of geography, history, and the sheer number of other lives being lived simultaneously. This connects to the Stoic cosmopolitan idea that human beings are, in Marcus's words elsewhere, "citizens" of a larger rational order (the kosmopolis), so seeing one's own troubles from that height is meant to reduce both excessive fear and excessive self-importance in the same motion — since from far enough above, neither one's suffering nor one's status looks quite as large as it feels up close.
Source: Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, Book 7.48
