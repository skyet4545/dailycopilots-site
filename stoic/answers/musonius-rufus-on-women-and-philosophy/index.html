What did Musonius Rufus teach about women and philosophy?

Musonius Rufus (c. 30-100 AD), a Roman Stoic teacher and one of Epictetus's own teachers, addressed the question directly in a lecture preserved under the title "That Women Too Should Study Philosophy." His opening argument rests on a claim about human nature rather than social convention: "Women as well as men have received from the gods the gift of reason, which we use in our dealings with one another and by which we judge whether a thing is good or bad, right or wrong." He extends the point to the body — both sexes have the same senses (sight, hearing, smell) and the same physical capacities, he argues, so if reason and perception are shared equally, the capacity for virtue must be shared equally too. From this premise, Musonius draws a conclusion that was unusual for his era: since philosophy is nothing more than "the pursuit of a good life" through the practice of virtue — self-control, justice, courage, wisdom — and since women are equally capable of practicing these virtues, women have equal need of philosophical training to develop them. He pushes back directly against the objection that philosophy is impractical or unbecoming for women managing a household, arguing instead that a philosophically trained woman would be a better household manager, more just, more self-controlled, and less swayed by extravagance or fear, precisely because philosophy trains the same virtues that domestic and civic life require. This lecture, along with his teaching that husbands and wives owed each other equal fidelity (rejecting the common double standard of his time), places Musonius among the more socially radical voices in the Stoic tradition, even though his broader ethics track closely with Epictetus and Seneca's.
Source: Musonius Rufus, Lectures, "That Women Too Should Study Philosophy" (trans. Cora E. Lutz)
