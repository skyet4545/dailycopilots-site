Top 25 Bible Verses About Revenge (With Explanations)

By Bible Copilot Updated October 5, 2026 Scripture-cited · editorial standards
Editorial note: Bible Copilot; reviewed by Codex source review. AI-assisted original paraphrases and study notes checked against linked World English Bible passages; no human scholarly review claimed.. Editorial standards.
Sources: World English Bible chapters linked at each of the25entries. Original paraphrases, not quoted Scripture.

Wanting to strike back after a wrong is understandable. These passages give several ways to examine that impulse. Some directly forbid personal retaliation. Some tell stories about people who could have retaliated but chose another course. Others address anger, speech, mercy, or fair judgment and are useful only when read in that wider context. The summaries below are original paraphrases and study notes, not Scripture quotations. Open each linked chapter to check the wording and surrounding passage for yourself.

1. Romans 12:17–20 — Leave personal revenge behind

Paraphrase: Paul tells believers not to return harm for harm. They should seek peace where possible, meet an enemy's needs, and leave vengeance to God. The passage gives a positive response to wrongdoing without promising an immediate outcome. Read Romans 12.

2. Proverbs 20:22 — Do not announce payback

Paraphrase: The proverb warns against deciding to repay a wrong yourself and calls for waiting on the Lord. It addresses the moment when anger becomes a plan for retaliation; it does not set a timetable for vindication. Read Proverbs 20.

3. 1 Peter 2:21–23 — Notice Christ's response

Paraphrase: Peter presents Jesus as an example: when mistreated, he did not answer with insults or threats, and he entrusted his case to a just judge. The point is his conduct amid suffering, not a claim that injury was acceptable. Read 1 Peter 2.

4. Matthew 5:38–42 — Respond differently to an enemy

Paraphrase: Jesus challenges the impulse to answer an injury with a matching injury. His examples move toward generosity rather than matching an injury. Read this as teaching about personal conduct; the passage does not erase every question about justice or safety. Read Matthew 5.

5. Romans 12:21 — Choose good over mirrored harm

Paraphrase: Paul closes the section by urging believers to answer evil with good. That is an active alternative to copying the wrong done to them. It names a way to respond, not a guarantee that the other person will change. Read Romans 12.

6. Hebrews 10:26–31 — Take divine judgment seriously

Paraphrase: Hebrews invokes God's right to repay within a grave warning about deliberate rejection of Christ. This is a warning about judgment, not a personal promise that every wrong against us will be settled in a way we can see. Read Hebrews 10.

7. Deuteronomy 32:34–36 — Hear vengeance within Moses' song

Paraphrase: The song places recompense in God's hands and also speaks of compassion for God's servants. Its setting concerns divine judgment of enemies and the people of the covenant. It should not be stretched into a ban on all human efforts to judge fairly. Read Deuteronomy 32.

8. Leviticus 19:17–18 — Reject grudges and address wrong

Paraphrase: Israel is told neither to take vengeance nor to nurse a grudge, and to love a neighbor. The preceding verse also speaks of correcting a neighbor. The passage allows wrongdoing to be named while forbidding a revengeful response. Read Leviticus 19.

9. Matthew 5:43–45 — Pray for an enemy

Paraphrase: Jesus calls his hearers to love their enemies and pray for those who mistreat them. This goes beyond merely withholding payback. It addresses the posture of the person who has been wronged. Read Matthew 5.

10. Proverbs 25:21–22 — Meet a need without using kindness as a weapon

Paraphrase: The proverb tells a person to give food or water to an enemy who needs it. Its difficult image about coals follows that instruction. The clear action is care for a need; it does not promise that kindness will defeat or humiliate the enemy. Read Proverbs 25.

11. Ephesians 4:25–32 — Be angry without letting anger rule

Paraphrase: Paul recognizes anger while warning against sin, bitterness, and harmful speech. Revenge is an application rather than the named subject of these verses. The surrounding call is to speak truthfully and build others up. Read Ephesians 4.

12. Colossians 3:12–14 — Practice forgiveness within a wider way of life

Paraphrase: The community is urged toward compassion, patience, forgiveness, and love. Forgiveness here answers complaints between people in a larger account of life together. It does not require pretending an injury did not happen. Read Colossians 3.

13. 1 Thessalonians 5:14–15 — Refuse evil for evil

Paraphrase: Paul tells the church to be patient with people and to prevent repayment of harm with harm. Instead, they should seek what is good for one another and for everyone. The contrast with retaliation is direct. Read 1 Thessalonians 5.

14. Genesis 50:15–21 — Joseph names the wrong and refuses payback

Paraphrase: After Jacob dies, Joseph's brothers fear he will repay their earlier evil. Joseph acknowledges what they did, refuses to take God's place as judge, and provides for their families. Mercy here does not depend on denying the harm. Read Genesis 50.

15. Proverbs 24:17–18 — Do not celebrate an enemy's fall

Paraphrase: This proverb warns against rejoicing when an enemy stumbles. Revenge can begin in the heart before anyone acts; the passage asks readers to examine that private pleasure. Later in the same chapter, another proverb rejects plans to return a wrong in kind. Read Proverbs 24.

16. 1 Samuel 24:10–15 — David spares Saul and seeks judgment

Paraphrase: Saul is hunting David when David has a chance to kill him. David spares him and asks God to judge between them. After Saul leaves, David returns to a stronghold. Refusing retaliation does not make the danger disappear. Read 1 Samuel 24.

17. Exodus 23:4–7 — Help an enemy while guarding justice

Paraphrase: Israel's law tells someone to return an enemy's wandering animal and help one that has fallen under a load. Nearby instructions also condemn false testimony and denial of justice. Doing good to an adversary and taking truth seriously belong together here. Read Exodus 23.

18. Proverbs 15:1 — Watch how a reply changes a conflict

Paraphrase: A gentle answer can turn anger aside, while harsh speech can stir it up. This proverb concerns escalation in conversation. It offers wisdom for a tense exchange, though it does not guarantee a gentle reply will make another person safe or fair. Read Proverbs 15.

19. Proverbs 19:11 — Know when to overlook an offense

Paraphrase: The proverb connects discretion with patience and honors the choice to overlook an offense. It speaks to some everyday wrongs; it is not a blanket instruction to conceal serious harm or avoid seeking help. Read Proverbs 19.

20. 1 Peter 3:8–12 — Answer insult with blessing

Paraphrase: Peter tells the community not to return evil or insult in kind, but to respond with blessing. The following lines call them to turn from evil and pursue peace. The promised blessing is not a timetable for visible reward. Read 1 Peter 3.

21. Psalm 37:7–9 — Do not let anger set your course

Paraphrase: The psalm addresses the frustration of seeing evildoers prosper. It urges patience before the Lord and warns that anger can lead to further wrong. It is guidance for the reader's response amid injustice, not a claim that injustice is harmless. Read Psalm 37.

22. Proverbs 24:28–29 — Do not plan to return the same wrong

Paraphrase: One saying rejects dishonest testimony against a neighbor; the next rejects the plan to do to another person what that person did to you. Read together, they oppose both false accusation and deliberate payback. Read Proverbs 24.

23. Micah 7:18–20 — Remember mercy without flattening judgment

Paraphrase: The book closes with praise for God's forgiveness and compassion toward the remnant of God's people. It offers a picture of mercy for reflection. It is not itself a direct command about revenge, nor does it say judgment never matters. Read Micah 7.

24. Nahum 1:1–8 — Hold patience and judgment together

Paraphrase: In an oracle concerning Nineveh, Nahum describes God as slow to anger and also as one who judges evil. The chapter holds those claims together. It does not give a personal schedule for when judgment will arrive after a particular injury. Read Nahum 1.

25. Titus 3:1–7 — Let remembered mercy shape your conduct

Paraphrase: Paul asks believers to be gentle and recalls that they too once lived in malice before receiving God's mercy. This is related guidance for humility toward others, not a direct revenge prohibition. Ask how remembering your own need for mercy affects your next action. Read Titus 3.

A short study exercise: Romans 12 and David's choice

Read Romans 12:17–21 and 1 Samuel 24:10–22, then make three short notes on paper or in your own notes app. You can use a fictional disagreement; no personal details are needed.

  1. Observe the instruction. In Romans 12, identify one response Paul forbids and one he encourages. Notice the qualifications about peace in verse 18. Which part concerns your own action rather than another person's response?
  2. Compare the story. What does David refuse to do in verses 10–12? Where do Saul and David go in verse 22? Keep what the narrative reports separate from advice you draw from it.
  3. Choose an application. Write one nonretaliatory step you could take and one outcome you cannot control. Consider whether distance or help is appropriate. The passages do not require every reader to respond to danger in the same way.

Worked example — original study notes, not Scripture quotations: Romans 12 rejects repayment of evil with evil and encourages practical good. David spares Saul, appeals for judgment, and later returns with his men to the stronghold while Saul goes home. An application might be to avoid sending an insulting reply while seeking appropriate help. That application is a possible response, not a command stated in either passage or a promise that the other person will change.

Frequently asked questions

Does refusing revenge mean I cannot seek justice? These passages distinguish personal payback from truthful action. Exodus 23 rejects false testimony and denying justice even while requiring aid to an enemy. Romans 12 addresses one's own retaliatory response. Read each passage for its setting rather than making one verse answer every justice question.

Must I stay close to someone who harmed me? 1 Samuel 24 shows David sparing Saul and then returning to a stronghold. His story does not impose one response on everyone, but it does show that refusing revenge and keeping distance can coexist.

Where should I begin? Start with Romans 12:17–21, which addresses repayment, peace, and doing good in one connected passage. Then read a narrative such as Genesis 50:15–21 to see a person name real harm while declining payback.
