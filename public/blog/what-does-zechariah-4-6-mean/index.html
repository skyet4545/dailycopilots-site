What Does Zechariah 4:6 Mean? Not by Might

📖 Bible Copilot ⏱ ~4 min read

Short answer: Zechariah 4:6 tells a discouraged governor that the temple will be finished by God's Spirit rather than by human strength or political muscle. It is a promise about how God's work gets done when the people doing it have almost no resources.

The context: a stalled building project

Zechariah prophesied to the community that had returned from Babylonian exile. They had begun rebuilding the temple in Jerusalem and then stopped — Ezra 4 describes opposition and a work halt lasting years, and Ezra 5:1–2 records Haggai and Zechariah stirring the leaders to resume.

The two leaders were Joshua the high priest and Zerubbabel, a descendant of David serving as governor. Zerubbabel had no army, no royal independence, and a population that remembered the first temple's glory and found the replacement humiliating (compare Ezra 3:12, where old men wept at the foundation).

Zechariah 4 comes as a vision: a golden lampstand with seven lamps, fed by two olive trees. The prophet asks what it means. The answer is verse 6.

What the verse means, phrase by phrase

"Then he answered and spake unto me, saying, This is the word of the LORD unto Zerubbabel, saying, Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, saith the LORD of hosts" (Zechariah 4:6, KJV).

"the word of the LORD unto Zerubbabel" — The message is addressed to a specific man with a specific job. That grounding matters: this is not an abstract slogan but a word to someone who had to show up at a construction site.

"Not by might, nor by power" — The two Hebrew words overlap. One suggests an army or a force of men; the other suggests strength or capacity. Together they cover the whole range of what a governor would normally rely on: manpower, wealth, political leverage, military backing. Zerubbabel had little of any of it.

"but by my spirit" — The alternative is God's own Spirit. In the vision's imagery, the lampstand does not need a servant to carry oil to it; the olive trees supply it directly (vv. 11–14). The picture is of a supply that does not depend on human labor to keep flowing.

"saith the LORD of hosts" — The title means the LORD of armies. It lands with deliberate irony in a verse that just dismissed armies: the one who dismisses human might commands every other force there is.

The verses around it confirm the reading. Verse 7 promises the "great mountain" of obstruction will become a plain and that Zerubbabel will bring out the headstone. Verse 9 says his hands will finish the house. And verse 10 asks, "For who hath despised the day of small things?" (KJV) — a direct answer to people embarrassed by a modest building.

Cross-references

  • Ezra 5:1–2, 6:14–15 — the historical record of the temple being finished.
  • Haggai 2:3–9 — the new temple's apparent smallness, and God's promise about its glory.
  • 1 Samuel 17:47 — "the LORD saveth not with sword and spear" (KJV).
  • Psalm 33:16–17 — no king is saved by a great army.
  • 2 Corinthians 12:9 — strength made perfect in weakness.
  • 1 Corinthians 2:4–5 — Paul's preaching not in persuasive words but in the Spirit's power.
  • Acts 1:8 — power tied to the Spirit's coming.

How to apply it today

The verse is frequently quoted as encouragement for anyone attempting something under-resourced, and that use is fair — the principle it states is general even though the address is specific.

Two clarifications keep it from becoming an excuse. First, "not by might" did not mean Zerubbabel stopped working. Verse 9 says his hands laid the foundation and his hands would finish it. The verse redistributes the credit for the outcome; it does not cancel the labor. Second, the promise attaches to a work God had commanded. It is not a blanket assurance that any project undertaken in faith will succeed regardless of wisdom or preparation.

There is genuine variation in how Christians apply the passage further. Some read the two olive trees and the lampstand as pointing to the offices of priest and king in Zerubbabel and Joshua, fulfilled ultimately in Christ; others emphasize the ongoing pattern of the Spirit empowering God's people, connecting it to Acts. The text supports the immediate meaning plainly, and both extensions are held by careful readers.

What is not in dispute: on the day the work looked smallest, God said the Spirit would be enough.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who was Zerubbabel? He was a descendant of David who served as governor of Judah after the return from Babylonian exile, and he led the rebuilding of the temple alongside Joshua the high priest. Ezra 3–6 and Haggai describe his role in the project.

Does "not by might, nor by power" mean I shouldn't work hard? No. Zechariah 4:9 says Zerubbabel's own hands would finish the house. The verse says the outcome does not ultimately depend on human strength or resources, not that human effort is unnecessary.

What do the lampstand and two olive trees represent? The vision shows a lamp supplied directly by two olive trees, an image of provision that does not run through human hands. Verses 11–14 call the trees "the two anointed ones," widely understood as pointing to Zerubbabel and Joshua in their governing and priestly roles, though interpreters differ on how far the symbolism extends.

What is "the day of small things" in verse 10? It refers to the discouraging modesty of the rebuilt temple compared to Solomon's, which had made some of the returning exiles weep (Ezra 3:12). God's rhetorical question rebukes the contempt for a small beginning He intended to complete.
