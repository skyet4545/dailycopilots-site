Matthew 11:28 in Greek: Rest, Burdens, and Jesus’ Invitation

By Bible Copilot Updated October 1, 2026 Scripture-cited · editorial standards

Quick answer

Matthew 11:28 invites those who labor and carry burdens to come to Jesus for rest. Verses 29–30 continue the invitation: take his yoke and learn from him. Read together, these verses connect rest with Jesus’ gentle instruction. They do not promise a life without work, grief or difficulty.

Read all three verses together

Verse 28 should not be separated from the invitation that follows. Jesus calls people to himself, speaks of his gentleness and humility, and describes his yoke and burden. The wider setting in verses 25–27 also directs attention to the relationship between the Father and the Son.

Three questions help trace the passage:

  1. Who is invited, and to whom are they invited to come?
  2. What does verse 29 ask people to do, and what does Jesus say about himself?
  3. How does verse 30 help you read “rest” alongside the image of a yoke?

Rest in verse 28 and verse 29

The SBL Greek New Testament has ἀναπαύσω (anapausō) in verse 28: the verb used in Jesus’ promise to give rest. Verse 29 uses ἀνάπαυσιν (anapausin), a noun form, in the promise of finding rest. These are related words in different grammatical roles. Calling the word in verse 28 simply “anapausis” obscures that distinction.

“I will give you rest” communicates the promise in ordinary English. A more awkward word-for-word rendering is not automatically more accurate. The surrounding sentence is more useful than claims that a Greek word hides a meaning English readers cannot access.

Who are the weary and burdened?

Verse 28 describes people who labor and are burdened. Those descriptions name the people Jesus invites; they do not list every cause of their burden. The image may prompt wider comparisons elsewhere in Matthew, but one related word cannot prove that only a single kind of burden is in view here.

Stay close to the wording before moving to application. Avoid turning the verse into a test that people must feel immediate relief to have responded faithfully. It is also not a substitute for practical help when someone is overwhelmed.

Why does Jesus speak of a yoke?

The invitation includes taking his yoke and learning from him. Rest therefore belongs alongside following and being taught by Jesus, not simply escaping every responsibility. The passage itself emphasizes his gentle and humble character. Begin there before adding illustrations or historical details that need separate evidence.

A practical way to study the invitation

Read verses 28–30 aloud. Notice the actions of coming, taking and learning, then notice what Jesus promises and says about himself. Write one question the paragraph raises for you. Compare interpretations with Scripture and trusted guidance rather than treating a word study as a complete answer to a difficult personal situation.
