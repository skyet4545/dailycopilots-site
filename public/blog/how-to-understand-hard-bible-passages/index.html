How to Understand Difficult Bible Passages (5 Steps)

📖 Bible Copilot ⏱ ~7 min read

To understand a difficult Bible passage, first diagnose why it's hard, then apply the move that matches. Most difficulty falls into five categories: (1) missing context — you're reading a verse without its paragraph; (2) genre — you're reading poetry, hyperbole, or apocalyptic imagery as though it were a police report; (3) cultural distance — the original readers knew a custom you don't; (4) an apparent contradiction with another passage; or (5) genuine obscurity, where the text is brief and even careful scholars disagree. Fix the first three and most "hard verses" stop being hard. The last two need a different posture, not a better search.

The practical five steps: read the whole surrounding chapter, identify the genre, check the historical setting, compare clear passages on the same subject, and only then consult a commentary. On tools, the best free options are Blue Letter Bible, Bible Hub, and StudyLight.org; a print study Bible (ESV Study Bible or NIV Cultural Backgrounds) solves cultural distance better than almost anything; Logos is the deepest paid platform. An AI study app can speed up the questions but shouldn't replace the reading.

Scripture itself concedes the problem. Peter, writing about Paul's letters: "In those, there are some things that are hard to understand, which the ignorant and unsettled twist" (2 Peter 3:16, WEB). Difficulty is expected; twisting is the danger.

Step 1: Read the whole chapter before the verse

Most misreadings are context failures, not translation failures. Read the paragraph before and after, then the chapter, then ask what the book as a whole is arguing. A verse that seems bizarre in isolation is often the obvious conclusion of the argument it sits inside. If you do only one thing from this list, do this one.

Step 2: Identify the genre

The Bible is a library, not one kind of book, and each genre has its own reading rules.

  • Narrative describes what happened; it does not always endorse it. Recorded ≠ recommended.
  • Poetry and wisdom use image, hyperbole, and generalization. Proverbs states what is usually true, not what is universally guaranteed.
  • Prophecy and apocalyptic work in symbol and pattern rather than plain chronology.
  • Epistles are occasional letters — real mail to real congregations with specific problems.
  • Law is embedded in a covenant with a particular people at a particular time.

"Pluck out your eye" is hyperbole in a sermon, not surgical instruction. Reading it that way isn't softening the text; it's reading it as written.

Step 3: Check the historical setting

Ask who wrote it, to whom, when, and why — then ask what the first readers knew that you don't: a legal practice, a household structure, a temple ritual, a local idol.

This is where cultural distance closes fast, and a study Bible with cultural notes does the work in a sentence. The NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible (Craig Keener on the New Testament, John Walton on the Old) exists for this exact problem; the ESV Study Bible is the better general-purpose option. Both run about $30–50 in print.

Step 4: Let clear passages interpret unclear ones

Not every passage is equally clear, and that's a feature you can use. Find several plain statements on the same subject and let them set the boundaries for the murky one. Cross-references — free in Bible Hub and Blue Letter Bible — are the cheapest reliable tool you own. Build doctrine on clear texts and read hard texts in their light; the reverse order leaves a whole system resting on one ambiguous verse.

Step 5: Consult commentaries — last, and more than one

Reading a commentary first tends to end the study rather than sharpen it. Read two or three after your own work, preferring ones that name competing views. If your sources all belong to one tradition, you'll mistake that tradition's consensus for the text's obvious meaning.

A worked example: 1 Peter 3:19

The text (World English Bible):

"Because Christ also suffered for sins once, the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring you to God; being put to death in the flesh, but made alive in the spirit; in which he also went and preached to the spirits in prison, who before were disobedient, when God waited patiently in the days of Noah, while the ship was being built." — 1 Peter 3:18–20

Diagnose. Category 5: genuine obscurity. Peter alludes in passing to something his readers evidently recognized and we don't.

Context and genre. The surrounding argument is about suffering unjustly and doing good anyway — an occasional letter using a compressed allusion mid-argument. Whatever verse 19 means, Peter deploys it to encourage a pressured church, not as a standalone doctrine of the afterlife.

Clear passages. The letter's plain claims — Christ suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring us to God — are the fixed points, and the obscure clause gets read inside them.

Commentaries. Careful interpreters hold at least three views: that Christ proclaimed victory to imprisoned fallen angels; that the preexistent Christ preached through Noah to that generation; or that Christ announced triumph to the dead of Noah's day. Each has real textual support.

Verdict. Hold the main point firmly and the mechanism loosely. Learning to say "the passage doesn't tell us" is a skill, not a failure.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the single most common reason a Bible passage seems confusing? Missing context. Reading a verse without its paragraph and chapter is the top cause by a wide margin, and the easiest to fix — read the whole chapter first.

What should I do when two passages seem to contradict each other? Check whether they address the same situation. Many apparent contradictions dissolve once you notice different audiences, covenants, genres, or time frames. If tension remains, note it honestly rather than forcing a resolution.

Do I need Greek or Hebrew to handle hard passages? No. You need a way to check a few key words, which Blue Letter Bible and Bible Hub provide free through Strong's numbers. Knowing the languages helps; not knowing them is not a barrier.

Can an AI Bible app explain a hard passage reliably? It can summarize the main interpretive options and speed up word studies, which is real help. It can also state a contested view with more confidence than the evidence supports. Treat any AI answer as a study partner's suggestion and check it against the cited text.

When should I accept that I don't know what a passage means? When the text is brief, the options are all defensible, and nothing in the context settles it — as in 1 Peter 3:19. Hold the clear teaching firmly and the disputed detail loosely.
