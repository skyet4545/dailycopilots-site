What Does Luke 11:9 Mean? Ask, Seek, Knock

📖 Bible Copilot ⏱ ~4 min read

Short answer: Luke 11:9 is Jesus's encouragement to pray persistently, given right after a parable about shameless persistence and right before a promise about the Holy Spirit. The three verbs urge continued asking, and the promise is anchored in the Father's goodness rather than in getting every specific request.

The context: a lesson on how to pray

Luke 11 opens with a disciple asking Jesus to teach them to pray. Jesus gives the prayer we call the Lord's Prayer (vv. 2–4). Then, instead of stopping there, He tells a small story.

In verses 5–8 a man knocks on a friend's door at midnight because unexpected guests have arrived and he has no bread. The friend is in bed with the household settled. Jesus says that even if friendship doesn't move him, "yet because of his importunity he will rise and give him as many as he needeth" (v. 8, KJV). "Importunity" translates a Greek word that carries the sense of shamelessness — the man keeps knocking past the point of politeness.

Verse 9 draws the conclusion. Then verses 11–13 finish the argument with a comparison: no father hands his son a stone or a serpent when he asks for bread or fish. "If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children: how much more shall your heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to them that ask him?" (v. 13, KJV).

What the verse means, phrase by phrase

"And I say unto you, Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you" (Luke 11:9, KJV).

"Ask ... seek ... knock" — The three verbs are in a tense that carries ongoing action; "keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking" is a fair rendering. That fits the midnight-door parable exactly. Jesus is not describing a single well-worded request but a pattern of persistence.

There is also a natural escalation. Asking is words. Seeking adds effort — you go looking. Knocking adds nerve — you stand at someone's door and make noise. The sequence intensifies.

"and it shall be given you" — The passive verbs ("shall be given," "shall be opened") point to God as the giver without naming Him, a common way of speaking reverently. The one who answers is the Father of verse 13.

What the promise covers — This is where careful reading matters. Verse 13 names the gift Jesus has in view: the Holy Spirit. (Matthew's parallel at 7:11 says "good things.") The passage's logic runs from a good human father to a better divine one, and its guarantee is that God gives what is genuinely good rather than a serpent dressed as a fish. It is not a promise that every request will be granted in the form requested — the same Gospel records Jesus praying in Gethsemane for a cup to pass and receiving something else (Luke 22:42).

Cross-references

  • Matthew 7:7–11 — the parallel saying, set inside the Sermon on the Mount.
  • Luke 18:1–8 — the persistent widow, another parable that "men ought always to pray, and not to faint" (KJV).
  • John 14:13–14; 16:23–24 — asking in Jesus's name.
  • 1 John 5:14–15 — confidence in asking "according to his will."
  • James 1:5–7 — ask for wisdom in faith.
  • James 4:3 — "ye ask, and receive not, because ye ask amiss" (KJV).
  • Luke 22:42 — "not my will, but thine, be done."

How to apply it today

The verse gives permission to be persistent without apology. That is the plainest takeaway, and for many people it is the needed one — Jesus told a story about a man being a nuisance and used it as a model.

The application goes wrong in two directions. Treated as a blank check, the verse gets used to promise outcomes God never guaranteed, and the fallout lands on people whose prayers weren't answered as asked. Treated too cautiously, it gets softened into a warning against expecting anything, which is the opposite of what a story about banging on a door at midnight teaches.

Held together with verse 13, it says: ask boldly and repeatedly, and trust the character of the one answering to sort out the form. Christians differ on how much unanswered prayer should prompt self-examination — James 4:3 raises motives, while Job and the Psalms are full of righteous prayers that went unanswered for long stretches — and that tension is best left honest rather than resolved with a formula.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Luke 11:9 mean God will give me anything I ask for? The passage promises that God gives good gifts, and verse 13 identifies the Holy Spirit as the gift in view. Other texts qualify the scope further — 1 John 5:14 mentions asking according to God's will, and James 4:3 mentions asking with wrong motives. Read together, the promise is about God's goodness rather than about guaranteed outcomes.

Why does Jesus use three verbs instead of one? Ask, seek, and knock escalate in effort and boldness, and all three are in a form suggesting continued action. The sequence reinforces the parable of the midnight visitor immediately before it: persistence is the point.

What does "importunity" mean in verse 8? It translates a Greek word carrying the sense of shamelessness — behavior that ignores social embarrassment. The neighbor gets up not because of friendship but because the man refuses to stop knocking.

How is Luke 11:9 different from Matthew 7:7? The wording is nearly identical, but the settings differ. Matthew places it in the Sermon on the Mount and concludes that the Father gives "good things"; Luke places it after the Lord's Prayer and the midnight-door parable and concludes with the Father giving the Holy Spirit.
