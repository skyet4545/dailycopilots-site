What Does Isaiah 61:1 Mean? The Verse Jesus Read Aloud

📖 Bible Copilot ⏱ ~4 min read

Short answer: Isaiah 61:1 announces a Spirit-anointed messenger sent to bring good news to the poor, heal the brokenhearted, and free prisoners. Jesus read this passage aloud in the synagogue at Nazareth and said it was fulfilled that day (Luke 4:16–21), which is why Christians read it as a description of His mission.

The context: restoration after exile

Isaiah 60–62 belongs to the section of the book concerned with Zion's restoration. The surrounding chapters describe a ruined city rebuilt, mourning reversed, and God's glory returning to a people who had lost nearly everything. Isaiah 61 opens with a first-person voice — someone commissioned and empowered to announce all of it.

Who that speaker is, in Isaiah's own setting, has been read more than one way. Some understand it as the prophet describing his own commission. Others connect it to the Servant figure who appears throughout Isaiah 42–53. Christians read it as ultimately messianic on the strength of Jesus' own use of it. These readings are not mutually exclusive, and it is worth holding the historical layer and the fulfillment layer together rather than collapsing one into the other.

What it means, phrase by phrase

The King James Version reads: "The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me; because the LORD hath anointed me to preach good tidings unto the meek; he hath sent me to bind up the brokenhearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives, and the opening of the prison to them that are bound" (Isaiah 61:1).

  • "The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me" — The authority claimed is not personal; it is delegated. Everything that follows flows from being Spirit-empowered rather than self-appointed.
  • "hath anointed me" — The Hebrew verb behind anoint gives us the word Messiah. Anointing marked kings, priests, and prophets as set apart for a commission.
  • "to preach good tidings unto the meek" — "Good tidings" is the Old Testament root that the Greek translation renders with the verb behind gospel. "The meek" (sometimes translated "the poor") refers to the humble and afflicted — those with no leverage.
  • "to bind up the brokenhearted" — Medical imagery, the language of bandaging a wound. Compare Psalm 147:3.
  • "to proclaim liberty to the captives" — The word for liberty recalls the Jubilee laws of Leviticus 25, when debts were released and people returned to their land. The announcement is not just comfort; it is release.
  • "the opening of the prison to them that are bound" — Literally opening — of eyes or of a prison, depending on how the phrase is read, which is why translations differ here.

Cross-references

  • Luke 4:18–19 — Jesus reads this passage in Nazareth. He stops partway through Isaiah 61:2, at "the acceptable year of the Lord," before the line about the day of vengeance — a detail many readers find significant.
  • Isaiah 42:1 — "I have put my spirit upon him" (KJV), the first Servant song.
  • Leviticus 25:10 — The Jubilee: "proclaim liberty throughout all the land" (KJV).
  • Psalm 147:3 — "He healeth the broken in heart" (KJV).
  • Matthew 11:5 — Jesus' answer to John the Baptist echoes this list: the blind see, the poor have the gospel preached to them.

How to apply Isaiah 61:1 today

Two things follow reasonably from the verse. First, it tells you who God's good news is aimed at: the humbled, the wounded, the trapped. If you feel disqualified by weakness, this passage names you as the target audience rather than the exception.

Second, it sets a pattern for those who follow the one who read it aloud. The mission described is concrete — news for the poor, healing for the brokenhearted, freedom for the bound. Christians have long differed on the balance between spiritual and material application here, with some emphasizing release from sin and others emphasizing tangible justice and mercy. The text lists both kinds of language side by side without ranking them, and both emphases have deep roots in Christian tradition.

Frequently Asked Questions

Did Jesus claim to be the speaker in Isaiah 61:1? In Luke 4:16–21 He reads the passage in the Nazareth synagogue, sits down, and says the Scripture is fulfilled in their hearing. Luke reports that the congregation understood the claim clearly enough to be provoked by it.

Why did Jesus stop reading mid-sentence? Luke's quotation ends at "the acceptable year of the Lord," leaving out Isaiah 61:2's "day of vengeance of our God." Many interpreters see this as marking the present phase of His mission as one of proclaiming favor, with judgment reserved for later; others are more cautious about reading intent into where the scroll was rolled up.

Who are "the meek" in this verse? The Hebrew term describes the afflicted, humble, and poor — people without power or standing. It carries both economic and spiritual overtones, which is why translations alternate between "poor," "afflicted," and "meek."

How does Isaiah 61 relate to Jubilee? The language of proclaiming liberty and release echoes the Jubilee legislation of Leviticus 25, when debts were canceled and property restored. Isaiah applies that imagery to God's restoration of an exiled people.
